## Summary

Zenobia (c. 240 – c. 274) was queen of the Palmyrene Empire in Roman Syria, ruling as regent for her young son Vaballathus after the assassination of her husband Odaenathus in 267. From the oasis city of Palmyra, she expanded her authority over Syria, Arabia, Egypt, and much of Anatolia, briefly controlling roughly a third of the Roman Empire's eastern territory.

Her rule matters because it represents the most serious challenge to Roman authority in the East during the Crisis of the Third Century. In 272, Zenobia declared her son emperor and herself Augusta, an open break with Rome. The emperor Aurelian defeated her armies at Antioch and Emesa, captured her at Palmyra, and paraded her in his triumph in 274. Her later reputation as a learned, ambitious, and cultured monarch has made her one of the most celebrated women of antiquity.

## Early Life & Background

Zenobia was born around 240 in or near Palmyra, a wealthy caravan city in the Syrian desert that had grown rich on trade between Rome and Persia. Her Palmyrene name was Bat-Zabbai, and inscriptions give her father's name as Zabbai. Palmyra's population spoke Aramaic, and its elite held Roman citizenship, so she grew up in a bilingual, culturally mixed society.

Ancient sources, particularly the unreliable *Historia Augusta*, describe her as educated in Greek, Egyptian, and Latin, and claim she studied under the philosopher Cassius Longinus. Later Arab and classical writers also credited her with a claim of descent from Cleopatra, though modern historians treat this as legend rather than genealogy.

Around 258 she married Odaenathus, the leading nobleman of Palmyra, as his second wife. Their son Vaballathus was probably born around 259 or 260.

## Rise to Prominence

Odaenathus rose to power after the Sasanian king Shapur I captured the Roman emperor Valerian in 260. He defeated Persian forces, restored order in the Roman East, and received the titles of *dux Romanorum* and "King of Kings" from Emperor Gallienus. Zenobia is recorded as accompanying him on campaign.

In 267, Odaenathus and his eldest son Hairan were assassinated, possibly at Emesa. Zenobia immediately secured the succession for the roughly ten-year-old Vaballathus and assumed the title "Queen" while acting as regent. She retained her husband's Roman titles for her son and consolidated the loyalty of the Palmyrene army and nobility.

## Key Achievements

- **Conquest of Egypt (270)**: Palmyrene forces under general Zabdas invaded Egypt with an army reported at 70,000 men, defeating the Roman prefect Tenagino Probus. This gave Zenobia control of Rome's grain supply.
- **Expansion into Anatolia**: By 271, her authority extended through Syria, Judaea, Arabia, and Asia Minor as far west as Ancyra (modern Ankara), with her troops reaching Chalcedon opposite Byzantium.
- **Imperial claim (272)**: Coins minted at Antioch and Alexandria named Vaballathus Augustus and Zenobia Augusta, an explicit assertion of imperial rank and a break with Emperor Aurelian.
- **Cultural patronage**: She maintained a court of scholars and philosophers, including Longinus, and practiced religious tolerance toward pagans, Jews, and Christians, reportedly protecting the bishop Paul of Samosata.

## Controversies & Criticism

Some ancient sources accused Zenobia of complicity in the murder of Odaenathus, alleging she wanted her own son to succeed over Hairan. No contemporary evidence supports this, and most modern historians reject the claim.

Her decision to claim the imperial title in 272 has been debated. Critics viewed it as overreach that guaranteed Roman retaliation, while defenders argue that Aurelian's campaign against her was already underway. After her capture in 272, the *Historia Augusta* alleges she blamed her advisors, including Longinus, whom Aurelian executed. Some historians consider this account hostile propaganda.

A Palmyrene revolt in 273 led Aurelian to sack the city, destroying much of its prosperity permanently. Whether Zenobia's ambitions were responsible for Palmyra's decline remains a point of scholarly discussion.

## Legacy & Influence

Zenobia's fate after 274 is disputed. One tradition says she died on the journey to Rome or was executed; another, widely repeated, holds that Aurelian spared her and she lived out her life in a villa at Tibur (Tivoli), marrying a Roman senator.

She became a symbol of female sovereignty and resistance across many cultures. She appears in Boccaccio's *De mulieribus claris* (1361–62), Chaucer's *The Monk's Tale*, Tomaso Albinoni's opera *Zenobia* (1694), and Rossini's *Aureliano in Palmira* (1813). In the 20th century, Syrian nationalists adopted her as a national heroine, and her portrait appeared on Syrian banknotes. Catherine the Great of Russia reportedly compared herself to her.

The destruction of Palmyra's monuments in 2015 renewed global attention on her city and her legacy as its most famous ruler.
