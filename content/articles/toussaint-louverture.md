## Summary

Toussaint Louverture (c. 1743 – 7 April 1803) was the foremost leader of the Haitian Revolution, the only slave uprising in history to produce an independent state. Born enslaved in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, he rose from coachman and plantation steward to general-in-chief of the colony and, by 1801, its governor for life.

Between 1791 and 1802 he outmaneuvered French, Spanish, and British forces in turn, abolished slavery in practice across the island, and issued a constitution that governed Saint-Domingue as an autonomous territory. His arrest and deportation by Napoleon in 1802 did not halt the revolution; his successor Jean-Jacques Dessalines declared Haitian independence on 1 January 1804, less than nine months after Louverture died in a French prison.

Louverture's career reshaped debates over slavery, race, and self-government in the Atlantic world, and he remains a foundational figure in Haitian national identity and Black political thought.

## Early Life & Background

Louverture was born on the Bréda plantation near Cap-Français in northern Saint-Domingue, probably in 1743. His father, Hyppolite, was reportedly an Allada nobleman from West Africa who had been sold into slavery. Toussaint was baptized François Dominique Toussaint and took the surname Bréda from the estate.

Unusually for an enslaved person, he learned to read and write, likely with help from his godfather Pierre Baptiste and Jesuit missionaries. He worked as a herdsman, coachman, and later steward, and was freed around 1776, when he was about 33. As a free man he rented land, briefly owned enslaved workers himself, and married Suzanne Simone Baptiste around 1782.

## Rise to Prominence

When the slave insurrection of August 1791 erupted in the north, Louverture joined it after a few weeks, initially as a physician and secretary to the rebel leader Georges Biassou. He soon commanded his own troops. In 1793 he allied with Spain, which controlled the eastern part of Hispaniola, and adopted the name "Louverture," meaning "the opening."

After the French commissioner Léger-Félicité Sonthonax proclaimed emancipation in 1793 and the National Convention abolished slavery on 4 February 1794, Louverture switched to the French side in May 1794. He was named brigadier general in 1795, lieutenant governor in 1796, and general-in-chief of the colony in 1797. He expelled the British in 1798, defeated his mixed-race rival André Rigaud in the War of Knives (1799–1800), and occupied Spanish Santo Domingo in January 1801.

## Key Achievements

- **Constitution of 1801**: Promulgated on 8 July 1801, it named him governor for life, abolished slavery permanently, and gave the colony effective autonomy while nominally remaining French.
- **Military command**: He built a disciplined army of roughly 20,000 men and defeated three European powers over a decade.
- **Economic revival**: His labor code kept plantations producing sugar and coffee through a system of paid but compulsory cultivation.
- **Diplomacy**: He negotiated trade agreements with Britain and the United States in 1798–1799 despite the ongoing war with France.

## Controversies & Criticism

Louverture's forced-labor regime, in which former slaves were bound to plantations under military supervision, drew resentment and revolts, including one in October 1801 led by his nephew Moïse, whom he had executed. His suppression of Rigaud's forces in the south involved reprisals against mixed-race residents. Critics also note that he owned enslaved people before the revolution and that his 1801 constitution concentrated near-absolute power in his hands.

## Legacy & Influence

Napoleon sent an expedition of roughly 20,000 troops under General Leclerc in February 1802. Louverture surrendered in May, was arrested in June, and died at Fort de Joux in France on 7 April 1803. Dessalines won independence the following year. Louverture inspired abolitionists such as William Wordsworth and Frederick Douglass, and C. L. R. James's 1938 history *The Black Jacobins* cemented his place in anticolonial thought. Haiti's international airport in Port-au-Prince bears his name.
