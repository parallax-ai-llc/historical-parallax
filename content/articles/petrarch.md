## Summary

Francesco Petrarca, known in English as Petrarch, was an Italian poet and scholar born in Arezzo on 20 July 1304. He is widely called the "Father of Humanism" for his recovery and study of classical Latin texts, and his vernacular lyric collection, the *Canzoniere*, fixed the sonnet as the dominant form of European love poetry for the next three centuries.

Petrarch's influence runs in two directions. As a scholar he rediscovered lost works of Cicero, championed classical Latin over medieval scholastic style, and framed the period between antiquity and his own day as a "dark age," a concept that shaped how later generations periodized history. As a poet he wrote 366 Italian poems addressed to a woman he called Laura, establishing conventions of idealized love that were imitated across Italy, France, Spain, and England.

He was crowned poet laureate in Rome on 8 April 1341, the first such coronation since antiquity, and died at Arquà near Padua on 19 July 1374, one day before his 70th birthday.

## Early Life & Background

Petrarch's father, Ser Petracco, was a Florentine notary exiled in 1302, the same year and faction as Dante Alighieri. The family moved in 1312 to Carpentras, near the papal court at Avignon. Petrarch studied law at Montpellier from 1316 and at Bologna from 1320 to 1323, but abandoned the subject after his father's death in 1326 and took minor clerical orders, which gave him income without heavy duties.

On 6 April 1327, by his own account, he first saw Laura in the church of Sainte-Claire in Avignon. Her identity remains uncertain, but she became the subject of his Italian poetry for the rest of his life.

## Rise to Prominence

Patronage from the Colonna family, particularly Cardinal Giovanni Colonna, supported his travels and book-hunting through the 1330s. In a letter dated 26 April 1336 he described climbing Mont Ventoux, a text often cited as an early expression of Renaissance self-awareness. He began his Latin epic *Africa*, on Scipio Africanus, around 1338, and it was largely on the strength of this unfinished work that he received the laurel crown on the Capitoline Hill in 1341.

In 1345, in the cathedral library at Verona, he found Cicero's letters to Atticus, a discovery that revealed the Roman statesman as a private, fallible man and shaped Petrarch's own habit of collecting his letters for posterity.

## Key Achievements

- **Canzoniere** (*Rerum vulgarium fragmenta*): 366 poems, mostly sonnets, revised until his death. Laura's death in the plague of 1348 divides the collection.
- **Latin prose**: the *Secretum*, an imagined dialogue with Saint Augustine; *De vita solitaria*; *De remediis utriusque fortunae*; and 24 books of *Familiar Letters*.
- **Trionfi**: an allegorical Italian poem in terza rima.
- **Scholarship**: he assembled one of the largest private libraries in Europe and in 1362 offered it to Venice, an early model for a public library.

## Controversies & Criticism

Petrarch spent 1353 to 1361 in Milan under the Visconti lords, whom he had earlier denounced as tyrants. Giovanni Boccaccio and other Florentine friends criticized this as a betrayal of republican ideals. His letters attacking the Avignon papacy as a new "Babylon" were withheld from circulation. Critics have also noted his ambivalence toward Dante, whom he claimed never to have read closely, and his inability to read the Greek manuscript of Homer he owned. He fathered two children, Giovanni in 1337 and Francesca in 1343, while holding clerical orders. In 2003, examination of his tomb showed the skull inside was not his.

## Legacy & Influence

The "Petrarchan sonnet," with its octave and sestet, became the template for Wyatt, Surrey, Sidney, and Spenser in England, and for Ronsard and Garcilaso on the continent. His classical program was carried forward by Coluccio Salutati and Leonardo Bruni, laying the groundwork for Renaissance humanism. His home village was renamed Arquà Petrarca in 1868.
