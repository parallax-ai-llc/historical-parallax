## Summary

On 30 July 2025 at 11:24 a.m. local time (23:24 UTC on 29 July), a magnitude 8.8 megathrust earthquake struck off Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula [^1][^2]. The US Geological Survey called it the most powerful earthquake since the 2011 Tohoku disaster and tied for the sixth strongest ever recorded [^2]. It produced a tsunami that flooded the Russian port of Severo-Kurilsk and led to warnings and evacuations around the Pacific Rim, from Japan and Hawaii to the Americas [^2][^3]. Its magnitude was enormous, but deaths were few, and many observers saw the event as a test of modern tsunami warning systems that the systems largely passed [^1][^3].

## Background

Kamchatka sits on the subduction boundary where the Pacific Plate dives beneath the Okhotsk microplate, one of the most seismically active zones in the world [^2]. The Russian Academy of Sciences said the quake was the strongest in the region since 1952 [^2]. A magnitude 7.4 foreshock struck about 60 km to the southwest on 20 July 2025 [^1].

## The Event

The USGS placed the epicenter about 119 km east-southeast of Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky at a depth of about 20.7 km [^2]. Wikipedia lists a revised depth of 35 km and a magnitude range of 8.7 to 8.8 [^1]. Strong shaking damaged buildings in Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky, including a kindergarten, and authorities declared a state of emergency [^2]. Waves of 3 to 4 meters reached the Kamchatka coast and flooded parts of Severo-Kurilsk in the northern Kuril Islands [^2][^3]. Wikipedia cites surveys reporting much higher local run-up in isolated spots [^1].

Across the Pacific, the US Tsunami Warning System issued warnings and advisories for Russia, Japan, Alaska, Hawaii, and the US West Coast [^3]. Japan ordered up to 1.9 million people to evacuate [^3]. Observed waves reached about 5.7 feet at Kahului, Hawaii, and 3.6 feet at Crescent City, California [^3]. Most warnings were later downgraded to advisories because the waves were smaller than forecast [^3].

## Response and Aftermath

Russian authorities kept a state of emergency in place in Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky while officials assessed the damage, which was moderate, in Kamchatka Krai and Sakhalin Oblast [^2][^1]. Shortly after the quake, Klyuchevskoy, Kamchatka's most active volcano, erupted [^1][^4]. Days later, Krasheninnikov volcano had what experts described as its first historically confirmed eruption in about 600 years [^4]. Aftershocks went on for months, and Wikipedia records a magnitude 7.8 event on 18 September 2025 as the largest [^1].

## Positive Perspectives

- Warning centers across the Pacific issued alerts within minutes, and millions of people moved to higher ground [^2][^3].
- No serious injuries or deaths were reported right after the quake, despite its size [^3].
- Wikipedia reports only one indirect death, in Japan, with most injuries occurring during evacuations [^1].
- Authorities scaled warnings back to advisories as wave data came in, which reduced disruption [^3].
- Monitoring of Klyuchevskoy and Krasheninnikov allowed aviation color codes to be issued promptly [^4].

## Negative Perspectives

- Severo-Kurilsk's port and fish-processing facilities flooded, and vessels were torn from their moorings [^1].
- Injuries were reported during hurried evacuations, including at Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky's airport [^2].
- Some regions were left uncertain because of conflicting messages, such as a warning for a later Kuril aftershock that Russia issued and then cancelled [^4].
- Large evacuation orders, including up to 1.9 million people in Japan, caused major disruption for a tsunami that turned out to be mostly modest [^3].

## Recent News

Wikipedia continues to track aftershocks and scientific analysis of the event. No major new damage findings for 2026 could be independently verified [^1].
