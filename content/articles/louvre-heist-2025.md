## Summary

On the morning of 19 October 2025, thieves disguised as workers used a truck-mounted lift to reach a window of the Louvre's Galerie d'Apollon. They broke in and stole eight pieces of the French Crown Jewels, which Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau said were worth about 88 million euros (roughly $102 million) [^1][^2]. The robbery took about seven minutes [^2]. Several suspects were later charged, but as of 2026 the jewels had not been recovered [^1][^3]. The theft led to scrutiny of security at the world's most-visited museum and, in February 2026, to the resignation of its director, Laurence des Cars [^3][^4].

## Background

The Galerie d'Apollon houses the remaining French Crown Jewels and other royal treasures [^1]. Before the heist, France's Court of Audit had already finished a review of the museum covering 2018-2024. It found that security modernization was moving at what audit chief Pierre Moscovici called a "woefully inadequate pace," and that work was not scheduled to start until 2026 or be completed until 2032 [^4]. The audit noted that about a third of rooms in the Denon wing, where the gallery is located, had no cameras [^4].

## The Event

Shortly after the museum opened, the thieves parked a truck with an extendable lift below the gallery and climbed up to a first-floor balcony. They cut through a window with power tools and smashed display cases [^2]. Wikipedia reports they spent about four minutes inside, with the whole operation taking under eight [^1]. They took eight objects. These included an emerald-and-diamond necklace that Napoleon I gave to Empress Marie-Louise, and a diadem of Empress Eugenie set with nearly 2,000 diamonds [^2]. The thieves fled on scooters and dropped Empress Eugenie's crown, which was recovered damaged [^2]. The museum closed for the day and reopened on 22 October [^1].

## Response and Aftermath

More than a hundred investigators were assigned to the case [^1]. Two men aged 34 and 39 from Aubervilliers were arrested within a week. One was stopped at Charles de Gaulle Airport with a one-way ticket to Algeria, and his DNA matched a scooter used in the getaway. Both were charged with organized theft and criminal conspiracy and partly admitted involvement [^5]. Prosecutors later charged a 37-year-old man and a 38-year-old woman, both of whom denied involvement [^5]. Wikipedia reports that five suspects had been charged in total [^1]. Interior Minister Laurent Nunez promised tighter security at cultural sites [^2]. The remaining crown jewels were moved to the Bank of France for safekeeping [^1].

## Positive Perspectives

- Investigators identified and arrested suspects within a week, using DNA and other forensic evidence [^5].
- The Empress Eugenie crown was recovered at the scene, although it was damaged [^2].
- The Court of Audit report and the public debate that followed put new pressure on museums to speed up security upgrades [^4].
- Macron's office called des Cars' resignation "an act of responsibility" intended to give the museum new impetus for security and modernization [^3].

## Negative Perspectives

- The eight stolen jewels are still missing [^1]. The prosecutor publicly warned the thieves against "the very bad idea" of melting them down, which would destroy their historic value [^2].
- The audit showed years of delays in security investment while the museum spent on more visible projects [^4].
- Labor unions criticized cuts to security staffing despite rising visitor numbers [^2].
- Wikipedia reports that a camera in the Apollo Gallery area was pointed the wrong way and that earlier audits had flagged weak passwords [^1].
- Des Cars faced other crises at the same time, including water damage to Egyptian antiquities and a staff strike [^3].

## Recent News

Laurence des Cars resigned on 24 February 2026, and President Emmanuel Macron accepted her resignation [^3]. Wikipedia reports that the Galerie d'Apollon reopened on 22 July 2026 and that the stolen jewels remained unfound as of August 2026 [^1].
