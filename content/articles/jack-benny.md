## Summary

Jack Benny (born Benjamin Kubelsky, February 14, 1894 – December 26, 1974) was an American comedian, actor and violinist whose career spanned vaudeville, radio, film and television. He is best remembered for *The Jack Benny Program*, which ran on radio from 1932 to 1955 and on television from 1950 to 1965, making him one of the few entertainers to dominate both media for more than three decades.

Benny built his comedy around a carefully constructed persona: a vain, penny-pinching man who was perpetually 39 years old, drove a decrepit Maxwell automobile, kept his money in an underground vault and played the violin badly. The character's flaws were the joke, and Benny was usually the target rather than the teller. His mastery of timing, especially the long pause and the deadpan stare, changed how comedy was performed on the air.

He is widely credited with pioneering the situation-comedy format, with a recurring ensemble cast and running gags that rewarded loyal listeners. Later comedians, including Johnny Carson, routinely cited him as a primary influence.

## Early Life & Background

Benny was born in Chicago, Illinois, and raised in nearby Waukegan. His father, Meyer Kubelsky, was a Polish-born Jewish immigrant who ran a saloon and later a haberdashery; his mother, Emma Sachs, was from Lithuania. He began violin lessons at age 6 and showed genuine talent, but he was an indifferent student and was expelled from Waukegan High School. By his mid-teens he was playing violin in the pit orchestra of Waukegan's Barrison Theater.

In 1911 he formed a vaudeville act with pianist Cora Salisbury, then toured with Lyman Woods. During World War I he served in the United States Navy at Great Lakes Naval Station, where his performances in a recruiting revue revealed that audiences responded more to his talking than to his playing.

## Rise to Prominence

After the war he adopted the stage name Ben K. Benny, then changed it to Jack Benny in 1921 to avoid confusion with bandleader Ben Bernie. Through the 1920s he became a polished vaudeville monologist, and in January 1927 he married Sadie Marks, who performed on his show as Mary Livingstone. His first film was *The Hollywood Revue of 1929*.

His radio debut came on Ed Sullivan's program on March 29, 1932, and his own series premiered on NBC on May 2, 1932. Sponsors over the years included Canada Dry, Jell-O and Lucky Strike. In 1949 he moved the show to CBS after William Paley's talent raid on NBC, and it transferred to television in 1950.

## Key Achievements

The radio and television programs featured a durable cast: Livingstone, announcer Don Wilson, bandleader Phil Harris, singer Dennis Day, and Eddie "Rochester" Anderson as Benny's valet. Recurring bits such as the mock feud with Fred Allen, begun in 1936, and the 1948 "Your money or your life" sketch, answered after a long pause with "I'm thinking it over!", became landmarks of American humor.

In film, his best-regarded work is Ernst Lubitsch's *To Be or Not to Be* (1942). He won Emmy Awards in 1957 and 1959, and *The Jack Benny Program* was named best comedy series in 1959. As a violinist he played benefit concerts with major symphony orchestras, including a 1957 Carnegie Hall appearance, raising several million dollars for musical institutions.

## Controversies & Criticism

In 1939 Benny pleaded guilty to smuggling jewelry into the United States without paying duty in a case involving Albert Chaperau; he was fined $10,000 and given a suspended sentence. The Rochester character has drawn criticism for relying on racial stereotypes in its early years, although the writers removed most of those traits after World War II, and Anderson's role was unusually prominent for a Black performer of the era. Benny's 1948 sale of his production company to CBS, structured as a capital gain, was also questioned as a tax maneuver.

## Legacy & Influence

Benny died of pancreatic cancer in Holmby Hills, Los Angeles, on December 26, 1974, at age 80, shortly before he was to star in *The Sunshine Boys*. He was inducted into the Television Hall of Fame in 1988. Waukegan named a middle school after him and erected a statue in his honor. His persona-driven ensemble comedy remains the template for the modern sitcom.
