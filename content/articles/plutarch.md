## Summary

Plutarch (c. 46–c. 119 CE) was a Greek biographer, essayist, and Platonist philosopher who lived under the Roman Empire and became one of the most widely read authors of antiquity. He is best known for the *Parallel Lives*, a series of paired biographies of famous Greeks and Romans, and for the *Moralia*, a collection of more than 70 essays and dialogues on ethics, religion, politics, and everyday life.

Writing in Greek during the reigns of emperors from Nero to Hadrian, Plutarch bridged two cultures. He treated Greek and Roman statesmen as moral case studies rather than as subjects of political history, and he wrote openly about the difference: "I am not writing history, but lives." His work preserved anecdotes and sources that would otherwise have been lost.

His influence extends far beyond classical scholarship. Shakespeare drew three Roman plays from Thomas North's 1579 English translation of the *Lives*, and figures from Montaigne to Emerson to the founders of the United States cited him as formative reading.

## Early Life & Background

Plutarch was born around 46 CE in Chaeronea, a small town in Boeotia in central Greece, roughly 100 kilometers northwest of Athens. His family was locally prominent and wealthy, and he remained attached to Chaeronea throughout his life, later joking that he stayed so that the town would not become smaller still.

He studied at Athens under the Platonist philosopher Ammonius, probably beginning around 66–67 CE, during the period when Nero toured Greece. His education covered mathematics, rhetoric, natural philosophy, and Platonic ethics. He married a woman named Timoxena, and the couple had at least five children, including a daughter who died in infancy and to whom Plutarch addressed a surviving consolation letter.

## Rise to Prominence

Plutarch traveled to Alexandria and made at least two visits to Rome, where he lectured on philosophy and formed friendships with influential Romans, including the consul Quintus Sosius Senecio, to whom he dedicated the *Parallel Lives*. He received Roman citizenship and took the family name Mestrius from his patron Lucius Mestrius Florus.

Despite these connections, he built his career in Greece. He held local magistracies in Chaeronea, and for roughly the last 30 years of his life he served as one of the two permanent priests of Apollo at Delphi, a position he held from about 95 CE. He wrote several dialogues about the sanctuary, including *On the Obsolescence of Oracles*. Later sources claim Trajan granted him consular rank and that Hadrian made him procurator of Achaea, though modern scholars treat both reports with caution.

## Key Achievements

The *Parallel Lives* originally contained 23 pairs of biographies, of which 22 pairs survive along with 4 unpaired lives. Subjects include Alexander and Julius Caesar, Demosthenes and Cicero, and Theseus and Romulus. Most pairs end with a formal comparison, or *synkrisis*, weighing the two men against each other.

The *Moralia* comprise 78 surviving works, ranging from practical advice such as *On Listening to Lectures* and *Advice on Marriage* to religious philosophy like *On Isis and Osiris* and polemics against Stoics and Epicureans. The 4th-century Lamprias Catalogue lists 227 titles attributed to him, suggesting that well over half of his output has been lost.

## Controversies & Criticism

Plutarch is not a reliable historian by modern standards. He freely used anecdote, rumor, and moralizing selection, and he sometimes reproduced errors in chronology from his sources. His Roman was imperfect by his own admission, and his readings of Latin authors occasionally show misunderstandings.

Some modern critics also note his conservative outlook, including conventional views on women and slavery, and his tendency to smooth over the violence of figures he admired. His essay *On the Malice of Herodotus* has been criticized as an unfair attack on a predecessor for failing to flatter the Greeks.

## Legacy & Influence

Plutarch was translated into French by Jacques Amyot in 1559 and into English by North in 1579. Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar*, *Antony and Cleopatra*, and *Coriolanus* follow North's text closely, sometimes almost line for line. Montaigne called him his constant companion, Rousseau read him as a child, and Beethoven cited him during his deafness.

The *Lives* remain a primary source for figures such as Solon, Themistocles, and Marius, whose other ancient biographies do not survive. His moral, character-centered approach shaped the genre of biography for nearly two millennia.
