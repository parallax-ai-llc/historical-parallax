## Summary

On 25 September 2025, young people in Antananarivo began protesting against chronic water and power cuts. Within three weeks the movement had grown into a nationwide challenge to President Andry Rajoelina [^1][^2]. The United Nations human rights office said at least 22 people were killed in the first days of unrest, and the government disputed that figure [^1][^3]. In mid-October the elite CAPSAT army unit sided with the protesters. Rajoelina fled the country and was impeached, and Colonel Michael Randrianirina took power [^1][^4]. The episode is one of several youth-led uprisings of 2025, and it ended in a military takeover that the African Union treated as a coup [^4].

## Background

Residents of the capital had faced power and water outages lasting up to 12 hours [^1][^2]. Protesters also pointed to corruption in public utilities and to falling living standards. About 75 percent of Madagascar's roughly 30 million people live below the poverty line [^1][^4]. Rajoelina first came to power in 2009 in a takeover backed by the same CAPSAT unit that would later turn against him [^5].

## The Event

The first protest on 25 September had been banned by the authorities. It turned violent as roadblocks went up, shops and banks were looted, and cable car stations were set on fire. Officials imposed a 7 pm to 5 am curfew in Antananarivo [^2]. The UN high commissioner for human rights, Volker Turk, said the protests had begun peacefully but that security forces had responded with "unnecessary force," including tear gas, beatings, and in some cases live ammunition. His office reported at least 22 dead and more than 100 injured. It said some of the deaths came from looting by gangs not linked to the protesters [^3].

Rajoelina dismissed his energy minister on 26 September and dissolved Prime Minister Christian Ntsay's government on 29 September [^1][^3]. On 6 October he appointed a general, Ruphin Zafisambo, as prime minister, but the protests continued [^1]. On 11 and 12 October, soldiers of the CAPSAT unit mutinied and urged troops not to fire on demonstrators [^1][^5]. On 13 October Rajoelina left Madagascar, reportedly aboard a French military aircraft [^5]. On 14 October the National Assembly voted to impeach him. Randrianirina then announced that the military was dissolving all state institutions except the lower house of parliament [^1][^5].

## Response and Aftermath

The African Union suspended Madagascar with immediate effect, and the UN expressed deep concern over what it called an "unconstitutional change of power" [^4]. Rajoelina condemned the takeover from abroad and refused to resign [^5]. Randrianirina was sworn in as president before the High Constitutional Court on 17 October 2025 and promised elections within two years [^4][^5]. On 20 October he appointed businessman Herintsalama Rajaonarivelo as prime minister. Gen Z activists said the choice was made without transparency or consultation [^1].

## Positive Perspectives

- The movement was organized largely by young people on social media and forced the government to respond within days. The energy minister was dismissed and then the whole cabinet [^1][^3].
- UN officials publicly condemned excessive force and called for restraint, which drew international attention to the protesters' safety [^3].
- Protest leaders and many residents welcomed the army's refusal to fire on crowds. One protest leader told Al Jazeera that the movement "was launched by the youth" and that the military supported it [^4].
- The new authorities published a timetable for a two-year transition, with national consultations, a new constitution, and presidential elections planned for late 2027 [^7].

## Negative Perspectives

- According to the UN rights office, security forces used live ammunition against protesters, and at least 22 people were killed. The Malagasy government disputed the figure [^1][^3].
- The African Union and the UN described the military's seizure of power as unconstitutional, and the AU suspended Madagascar from the bloc [^4].
- Gen Z activists criticized the appointment of a businessman as prime minister without consultation, and some questioned whether the transition reflected their demands [^1][^6].
- Rajoelina never accepted his removal, and the process that replaced an elected president with a military officer drew lasting legal and diplomatic disputes [^5].

## Recent News

In late February 2026 the transitional authorities published a 24-month "refoundation" plan. It includes national consultations through 2026, a new constitution, and presidential elections in late 2027 [^7]. On 11 March 2026 Randrianirina dismissed Prime Minister Rajaonarivelo and the whole cabinet without giving a public reason [^6]. A spokesperson for the youth movement warned at the time that the regime might be "drifting" away from the protesters' demands [^6]. On 16 March he named Mamitiana Rajaonarison, the head of the country's financial intelligence unit, as prime minister [^7].
