## Summary

Medgar Wiley Evers (July 2, 1925 – June 12, 1963) was an American civil rights activist and the first field secretary of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) in Mississippi. A World War II veteran, he spent the last decade of his life organizing voter registration drives, investigating racially motivated killings, and challenging segregation in the state widely regarded as the most hostile to Black civil rights in the United States.

Evers was assassinated outside his home in Jackson, Mississippi, on June 12, 1963, hours after President John F. Kennedy delivered a nationally televised address on civil rights. His killer, white supremacist Byron De La Beckwith, was not convicted until 1994, more than three decades after the murder.

Evers's death galvanized national support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964, and his life has become a defining symbol of the cost and courage of the movement in the Deep South.

## Early Life & Background

Evers was born in Decatur, Mississippi, the third of five children of James and Jessie Evers, who owned a small farm and worked at a sawmill. He walked 12 miles each way to attend a segregated school and was deeply affected as a boy by the lynching of a family friend, Willie Tingle, whose bloodied clothes were left hanging near the road for months.

In 1943 Evers was inducted into the United States Army and served in a segregated unit in the European theater, taking part in the Normandy campaign. He was honorably discharged as a sergeant in 1946.

Using the G.I. Bill, he enrolled at Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College (now Alcorn State University), where he studied business administration, played football, ran track, and edited the campus newspaper. He married classmate Myrlie Beasley on December 24, 1951, and graduated in 1952.

## Rise to Prominence

After college Evers sold insurance for the Magnolia Mutual Life Insurance Company in Mound Bayou, an all-Black town in the Mississippi Delta. Traveling through the Delta, he witnessed the extreme poverty of sharecroppers and began organizing local NAACP chapters and boycotts of gas stations that denied Black customers use of restrooms.

In early 1954 Evers applied to the University of Mississippi School of Law. His application was rejected on racial grounds, and the case drew the attention of the NAACP's national office. On December 15, 1954, the organization named him its first field secretary for Mississippi, based in Jackson.

## Key Achievements

- **Building the Mississippi NAACP**: Evers expanded membership, founded new local chapters, and recruited young activists across the state under constant threat of violence.
- **Emmett Till investigation (1955)**: He helped investigate the murder of 14-year-old Emmett Till, secretly gathering witnesses and evidence while disguised as a farm worker.
- **Desegregating Ole Miss (1962)**: Evers advised and supported James Meredith through the legal fight that made Meredith the first Black student admitted to the University of Mississippi in October 1962.
- **Jackson campaign (1962–1963)**: He organized boycotts of downtown white merchants, sit-ins, and voter registration efforts. On May 20, 1963, he delivered a televised address on WLBT demanding an end to segregation in Jackson.

## Controversies & Criticism

Evers was viewed as a dangerous radical by white Mississippi authorities, and the state-funded Mississippi State Sovereignty Commission kept him under surveillance. Within the movement, he sometimes faced tension with the NAACP's cautious national leadership, which preferred litigation over the direct-action tactics that Evers increasingly embraced alongside student activists.

The greatest controversy surrounds the failure of justice after his death. Beckwith was tried twice in 1964; both all-white juries deadlocked. Later revelations that the Sovereignty Commission had assisted the defense in screening jurors contributed to the case being reopened. Beckwith was finally convicted of murder on February 5, 1994, and died in prison in 2001.

## Legacy & Influence

Evers was buried with full military honors at Arlington National Cemetery on June 19, 1963, before a crowd of more than 3,000. Bob Dylan's "Only a Pawn in Their Game" and Nina Simone's "Mississippi Goddam" responded directly to his murder.

Myrlie Evers-Williams carried on his work and served as chair of the NAACP from 1995 to 1998. Medgar Evers College in Brooklyn, New York, was founded in 1970, and the Jackson airport was renamed Jackson–Medgar Wiley Evers International Airport in 2004. His home in Jackson was designated a National Monument in 2019. Evers was posthumously awarded the NAACP's Spingarn Medal in 1963, and the 1996 film *Ghosts of Mississippi* dramatized the final trial.
