## Summary

Since late February 2026, Pakistan and Taliban-ruled Afghanistan have fought their most sustained conflict since the Taliban returned to power in 2021 [^1]. It began with Pakistani air strikes on alleged militant camps in eastern Afghanistan. After Taliban forces attacked Pakistani border posts, Pakistan's defence minister declared "open war" on 27 February and bombed Kabul and Kandahar [^1][^2]. The fighting has combined air strikes and artillery duels along the Durand Line with a growing Pakistani Taliban (TTP) insurgency inside Pakistan [^1]. It has displaced about 100,000 people in Afghanistan and produced heavily disputed casualty claims, including a deadly strike on a Kabul drug treatment hospital [^1][^3][^4].

## Background

Afghanistan has never formally recognised the Durand Line as its border with Pakistan. Islamabad accuses the Taliban of sheltering the TTP, which the Taliban deny [^1]. Clashes in October 2025 ended in a fragile Qatar-mediated ceasefire, but later talks in Doha and Istanbul did not produce a lasting deal [^1][^2]. In early 2026 Pakistan suffered major attacks, including a 6 February suicide bombing at a Shia mosque in Islamabad that killed 31 worshippers and was claimed by Islamic State-Khorasan [^1]. On 11 February Defence Minister Khawaja Asif warned that Pakistan might act before Ramadan [^1].

## The Event

On the night of 21 to 22 February, Pakistani aircraft struck what Islamabad called seven TTP and Islamic State camps in Nangarhar, Paktika, and Khost. The Taliban said civilians were killed, including 18 members of one family, and UNAMA confirmed more than 13 civilian deaths [^1]. On 26 February Taliban forces launched a "retaliatory operation" against Pakistani posts along the border [^1][^2]. On 27 February Asif wrote, "Now it is open war between us and you." Pakistan's Operation Ghazab lil-Haq hit Taliban targets in Kabul, Kandahar, and Paktia, including an ammunition depot near Darulaman in Kabul [^1][^2]. The two sides issued competing claims of hundreds of enemy fighters killed. The TTP's leader ordered his fighters to step up attacks inside Pakistan [^1].

On the night of 16 March, an air strike destroyed the Omid drug addiction treatment hospital in Kabul. Taliban officials said more than 400 people were killed, while Pakistan said it had hit only military installations [^3]. Al Jazeera later reported that the UN had documented 143 deaths in the strike [^5].

## Response and Aftermath

At the request of Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Turkey, both sides observed a pause for Eid al-Fitr from 19 to 24 March [^5]. Pakistan warned that operations would "resume with renewed intensity" after any attack, and shelling continued in eastern provinces during and after the holiday [^4][^5]. China hosted working-level talks in Urumqi in early April, and Beijing said both sides had agreed to work toward easing tensions [^1]. The UN Security Council urged Afghanistan to combat terrorism and extended UNAMA's mandate. The US voiced support for Pakistan, and India condemned the strikes [^1][^3].

## Positive Perspectives

- Mediation by Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Turkey produced the Eid al-Fitr pause, and Qatar called it "a positive gesture" toward de-escalation [^5].
- China-hosted talks in April led to a period of lower-intensity fighting from April to late May [^1].
- A shorter Eid al-Adha truce announced by the TTP in late May briefly reduced violence [^1].
- UN agencies, including OCHA and UNAMA, documented civilian harm and displacement, which gives an independent record of a conflict marked by censorship [^1][^4].

## Negative Perspectives

- According to OCHA, cross-border shelling and air strikes had killed or injured several hundred civilians by early April. About 94,000 people had been displaced in five provinces, and 25 health facilities and 41 schools were damaged or closed [^4].
- The strike on the Kabul addiction hospital became the deadliest and most contested incident of the war. The UN, the Taliban, and Pakistan gave very different accounts [^3][^5].
- Taliban intelligence ordered Afghan media not to report strike locations or Taliban casualties, which makes independent verification difficult [^1].
- The war fed a wider insurgency inside Pakistan, including the September attack on Kohat's police headquarters that killed at least 31 people [^1][^6].

## Recent News

On 18 September 2026, militants from Ittehad-ul-Mujahideen Pakistan, a TTP-linked faction, stormed the Old Police Lines complex in Kohat, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. They killed at least 31 people, including 16 police personnel, and injured more than 100 [^6]. Pakistan blamed Afghanistan and carried out new air strikes in Kunar and Paktika on 21 September and on 10 sites on 24 September. The Taliban reported civilian deaths and vowed to respond [^1].
