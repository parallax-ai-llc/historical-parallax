## Summary

Zora Neale Hurston (January 7, 1891 – January 28, 1960) was an American writer, anthropologist, and filmmaker whose work documented African American folk culture in the rural South and the Caribbean. She published four novels, two books of folklore, an autobiography, and more than 50 short stories, essays, and plays. Her best-known novel, *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937), is now a fixture of American literature curricula.

Hurston was a central figure of the Harlem Renaissance in the 1920s, yet she died in poverty and relative obscurity in 1960. Her reputation was revived in the 1970s, largely through the efforts of novelist Alice Walker, and she is today regarded as one of the most important American writers of the twentieth century.

Her significance rests on two linked contributions: she treated Black vernacular speech and folk tradition as serious literary and scientific material, and she did so as a trained anthropologist who collected the material herself.

## Early Life & Background

Hurston was born in Notasulga, Alabama, the fifth of eight children of John Hurston, a Baptist preacher and carpenter, and Lucy Ann Potts, a schoolteacher. In 1894 the family moved to Eatonville, Florida, one of the first incorporated all-Black towns in the United States. Her father served as mayor there, and Hurston later presented Eatonville as her true hometown and the setting for much of her fiction.

Her mother died in 1904, when Hurston was 13. Her father remarried, and Hurston was sent to boarding school in Jacksonville before leaving to work as a maid with a traveling Gilbert and Sullivan troupe. In 1917 she enrolled at Morgan Academy in Baltimore, claiming to be 16 rather than 26 so she could qualify for free public schooling. She kept the revised birth year for the rest of her life.

She studied at Howard University from 1918 to 1924, co-founding the student newspaper *The Hilltop*. In 1925 she moved to New York and entered Barnard College on a scholarship as its only Black student, studying anthropology under Franz Boas. She graduated in 1928 and pursued graduate work at Columbia University.

## Rise to Prominence

Hurston arrived in Harlem in 1925 as the literary movement there was gathering force. Her story "Spunk" and play *Color Struck* won prizes in the *Opportunity* magazine contest that year. In 1926 she co-edited the single-issue magazine *Fire!!* with Langston Hughes and Wallace Thurman.

From 1927 she conducted fieldwork in Florida, Alabama, and New Orleans, funded partly by patron Charlotte Osgood Mason, who supported her from 1927 to 1932. Hurston recorded work songs, folktales, sermons, and hoodoo practices, and shot 16 mm film footage of her subjects, which survives as some of the earliest ethnographic film by a Black American.

A Guggenheim Fellowship in 1936 and 1937 took her to Jamaica and Haiti, where she wrote *Their Eyes Were Watching God* in about seven weeks.

## Key Achievements

- *Jonah's Gourd Vine* (1934), her first novel, drawing on her parents' marriage
- *Mules and Men* (1935), the first major collection of African American folklore compiled by a Black researcher
- *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937), her most celebrated novel
- *Tell My Horse* (1938), an account of Haitian and Jamaican vodou
- *Moses, Man of the Mountain* (1939)
- *Dust Tracks on a Road* (1942), an autobiography that won the Anisfield-Wolf Book Award
- *Seraph on the Suwanee* (1948), her last published novel
- *Barracoon*, an interview with Cudjo Lewis, a survivor of the last known slave ship to the United States, written in 1931 and published in 2018

## Controversies & Criticism

Richard Wright reviewed *Their Eyes Were Watching God* harshly in 1937, arguing that its dialect writing pandered to white readers and carried "no theme, no message." Other Harlem Renaissance figures, including Alain Locke, thought her work insufficiently engaged with racial protest.

Her politics drew criticism as well. She was a self-described conservative who opposed the New Deal and, in a 1955 letter to the *Orlando Sentinel*, objected to the *Brown v. Board of Education* ruling, arguing that forced integration implied Black schools were inferior. Her collaboration with Langston Hughes on the play *Mule Bone* ended in a bitter 1931 dispute over authorship. In 1948 she was falsely accused of molesting a boy; the charge was dismissed, but the publicity damaged her career.

## Legacy & Influence

Hurston spent her last years in Fort Pierce, Florida, working as a substitute teacher and maid, and died of hypertensive heart disease at the St. Lucie County Welfare Home. She was buried in an unmarked grave. In 1973 Alice Walker located the approximate site and placed a headstone reading "A Genius of the South." Walker's 1975 essay in *Ms.* magazine triggered a wave of reprints and scholarship.

*Their Eyes Were Watching God* has sold millions of copies and was adapted for television in 2005. Eatonville hosts the annual Zora Neale Hurston Festival of the Arts and Humanities, founded in 1990. The Library of America published her collected works in 1995, and her home in Fort Pierce is a National Historic Landmark.
