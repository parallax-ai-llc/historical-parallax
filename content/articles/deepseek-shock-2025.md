## Summary

The "DeepSeek shock" refers to the reaction in global technology markets after the Chinese start-up DeepSeek released its R1 reasoning model on 20 January 2025 [^1]. Within a week its free chatbot app overtook ChatGPT as the most downloaded free app on Apple's U.S. App Store [^1]. On 27 January 2025 Nvidia shares fell about 17 percent, erasing roughly $589 billion in market value, the largest one-day loss for any company in U.S. stock-market history [^2]. The episode challenged assumptions that frontier artificial intelligence required ever-larger spending on advanced chips [^2].

## Background

DeepSeek is based in Hangzhou and is owned and funded by the hedge fund High-Flyer; its founder and chief executive is Liang Wenfeng [^1]. In December 2024 the company released DeepSeek-V3, reporting that the final training run used about 2.788 million H800 GPU hours, an estimated $5.576 million, on a cluster of 2,048 Nvidia H800 chips [^3]. The company noted that the figure excluded earlier research and experiments, and some outside researchers argued total development costs were much higher [^3][^4].

## The Event

R1 was released on 20 January 2025 under the permissive MIT License, the same day as President Donald Trump's inauguration, and initially drew limited attention [^1][^5]. Over the following weekend the DeepSeek assistant climbed to the top of Apple's U.S. App Store [^5]. Investor Marc Andreessen wrote that "DeepSeek R1 is AI's Sputnik moment" [^5]. On Monday 27 January Nvidia fell about 17 percent, Broadcom fell 17.4 percent, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 3.1 percent [^2]. Nvidia's loss was more than double the previous one-day record, which Nvidia itself had set in September 2024 [^2].

## Response and Aftermath

President Trump said the release "should be a wake-up call for our industries," while suggesting cheaper AI could ultimately benefit U.S. companies [^5]. Regulators acted on privacy grounds: Italy's data protection authority ordered DeepSeek's chatbot blocked, and South Korea suspended new downloads of the app in February 2025 [^6]. Australia barred DeepSeek products from government systems, and the U.S. National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal 2026 directed its removal from defense and intelligence devices [^1]. In a paper published in Nature in September 2025, DeepSeek said R1's reasoning training cost about $294,000, using 512 H800 chips [^7].

## Positive Perspectives

- Supporters said R1's open MIT-licensed weights let researchers and companies worldwide study and build on a frontier-level reasoning model [^1].
- The efficiency claims pushed the industry to consider cheaper training and inference methods, an outcome Trump described as potentially "positive" for U.S. firms [^5].
- DeepSeek's Nature paper, co-authored by Liang Wenfeng, gave the first peer-reviewed account of how R1 was trained and what it cost [^7].
- Nvidia had recovered almost all of its DeepSeek losses by late February 2025, as large technology companies kept their AI infrastructure spending plans [^8].

## Negative Perspectives

- Analysts warned that the headline $5.6 million figure covered only the final V3 training run, not research, staff or hardware costs [^3][^4].
- Privacy regulators in Italy and South Korea found problems with how DeepSeek collected and shared user data [^6].
- Several governments, including Australia and the United States, restricted DeepSeek on official devices over security concerns [^1].
- In February 2026 Anthropic accused DeepSeek of using thousands of fraudulent accounts to generate conversations with its Claude models for training, an allegation that renewed debate about distillation [^1].
- The record selloff showed how concentrated U.S. stock-market gains had become in a handful of AI-linked companies [^2].

## Recent News

DeepSeek released V3.1 in August 2025 and V3.2 in December 2025, followed by a V4 preview on 24 April 2026 and official V4 versions in mid-2026 [^1].
