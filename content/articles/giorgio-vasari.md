## Summary

Giorgio Vasari (1511–1574) was an Italian painter, architect, and writer whose book *Le Vite de' più eccellenti pittori, scultori e architettori* ("The Lives of the Most Excellent Painters, Sculptors, and Architects") is widely regarded as the founding text of art history. First published in Florence in 1550 and greatly expanded in 1568, the *Lives* organized the Italian Renaissance into a narrative of progress that culminated in Michelangelo.

As an artist, Vasari served the Medici court in Florence for decades. He designed the Uffizi, painted the vast fresco cycles in the Palazzo Vecchio, and built the elevated Vasari Corridor linking the Palazzo Vecchio to the Palazzo Pitti. He also helped found the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno in 1563, one of the first formal art academies in Europe.

His writing shaped how later generations understood the period, popularizing the very term "Renaissance" (*rinascita*) and fixing the reputations of artists from Giotto to Titian.

## Early Life & Background

Vasari was born on 30 July 1511 in Arezzo, Tuscany, into a family of potters and craftsmen. He received an early education in Arezzo, where the French glass painter Guillaume de Marcillat is said to have given him instruction. Around 1524, Cardinal Silvio Passerini brought him to Florence, where he studied alongside the young Medici heirs Ippolito and Alessandro and trained in the workshops associated with Andrea del Sarto and Baccio Bandinelli.

The Medici expulsion of 1527 and his father's death from plague the same year interrupted his progress, leaving Vasari responsible for supporting his siblings. He worked in Arezzo, Florence, and Rome during the late 1520s and early 1530s, studying antique sculpture and the works of Raphael and Michelangelo.

## Rise to Prominence

Vasari's early patrons included Duke Alessandro de' Medici, for whom he painted portraits and decorative works until the duke's assassination in 1537. He then spent several years travelling and working for a range of patrons in Bologna, Venice, Naples, and Rome. In Rome he executed the fresco decoration of the Sala dei Cento Giorni in the Palazzo della Cancelleria in 1546, reportedly completed in 100 days.

In 1554 Duke Cosimo I de' Medici brought Vasari permanently into Florentine service. He became the duke's chief artistic and architectural adviser, a position he held until his death in 1574.

## Key Achievements

- **The *Lives***: The 1550 edition covered artists from Cimabue onward; the 1568 edition added living artists, a section on Venetian painting, and Vasari's own autobiography. It remains a primary source for the period.
- **The Uffizi**: Begun in 1560 as offices for the Florentine magistrates, the building is now one of the world's most visited art museums.
- **The Vasari Corridor**: Built in 1565 in roughly five months for the wedding of Francesco I de' Medici, the enclosed passage runs about one kilometre across the Ponte Vecchio.
- **Palazzo Vecchio**: Vasari remodelled the interior and painted the Salone dei Cinquecento with monumental battle scenes celebrating Florentine victories.
- **Florence Cathedral dome**: He began the *Last Judgement* fresco inside Brunelleschi's dome in 1572. Federico Zuccari completed it after his death.
- **Accademia delle Arti del Disegno**: Founded in 1563 under Cosimo's patronage, with Vasari as a principal organizer.

## Controversies & Criticism

Vasari's reliability as a historian has long been questioned. He relied on hearsay for artists he never met, invented or embellished anecdotes, and freely revised dates and attributions between the two editions. His Florentine bias led him to undervalue Venetian and northern artists and to treat Michelangelo as the endpoint of art. Some of his claims, such as Andrea del Castagno murdering Domenico Veneziano, are demonstrably false.

As a painter, he has often been judged a competent but derivative Mannerist whose work prioritized speed and scale over invention. The Palazzo Vecchio remodelling also destroyed or covered earlier works, including possibly Leonardo's unfinished *Battle of Anghiari*, a loss still debated today.

## Legacy & Influence

Vasari's biographical model, tracing an artist's life alongside their works, dominated art writing for centuries and influenced authors such as Karel van Mander and Giovanni Baglione. His concept of the Renaissance as a rebirth of classical excellence, progressing through distinct stages, still frames introductory histories of the period. The Uffizi and the Vasari Corridor remain central to Florence's cultural landscape, and the Accademia he helped found continues to operate.
