## Summary
At 07:58 local time on 3 April 2024, a powerful earthquake struck off Hualien County on Taiwan's east coast, measured at ML 7.2 by Taiwan's Central Weather Administration and Mw 7.4 by the US Geological Survey [^1]. It was the strongest earthquake to hit Taiwan since the 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake, which killed more than 2,000 people [^2]. Wikipedia's final tally lists 19 deaths, 1,147 injuries, and one person missing, with many casualties caused by rockfalls in Taroko National Park [^1]. The comparatively low toll drew international attention to Taiwan's building codes and preparedness [^2].

## Background
Taiwan lies at the junction of the Philippine Sea and Eurasian plates and experiences frequent earthquakes [^1]. After the 1999 Chi-Chi disaster exposed weak construction, Taiwan spent the following 25 years retrofitting bridges and buildings, tightening earthquake-resistant building codes, holding regular drills, and building an alert system, according to NPR [^2].

## The Event
The mainshock struck at a depth of about 40 km, and shaking reached intensity 6+ on Taiwan's scale [^1][^3]. In Hualien City, the Uranus Building, a mixed-use high-rise with a weak ground-floor frontage, partly collapsed and tilted sharply; occupants were rescued from it [^1]. Landslides and rockfalls blocked mountain roads and highways, and about 600 people were stranded in and around Taroko Gorge, including twelve park visitors, two of them Canadian nationals, who were trapped along a trail [^1]. The most severe damage in the park occurred around the Swallow Grotto and Shakadang Trail, where five members of one family were killed by falling rocks [^4]. More than 1,700 aftershocks were recorded by November 2024, including events of magnitude 6.4 and 6.1 [^1].

## Response and Aftermath
Rescuers worked over the following days to reach people stranded behind landslides and to recover those trapped [^1]. At least 47 countries offered assistance; Japan pledged $1 million and South Korea $500,000, according to Wikipedia [^1]. The government estimated that restoring Taroko National Park would take about seven years and cost NT$3 billion, and Premier Cho Jung-tai pledged regular visits to monitor reconstruction [^4]. Tourism suffered, with hotels in Taroko's Tianxiang area closed until October 2024 [^5].

## Positive Perspectives
- NPR reported that most buildings in Hualien remained standing, an outcome engineers attributed to post-1999 code reforms and retrofitting [^2].
- Northeastern University scholar Daniel Aldrich called the low casualty count "quite remarkable" given the quake's magnitude, in remarks to NPR [^2].
- Regular earthquake drills and school retrofits were cited as reasons for the effective public response [^2].
- Dozens of countries offered aid, and rescue crews reached hundreds of stranded people in difficult mountain terrain [^1].
- Hotels in Taroko's Tianxiang area reopened in October 2024 with government tourism subsidies [^5].

## Negative Perspectives
- Taiwan's early warning system did not issue an advance alert for the mainshock, and its magnitude was initially underestimated at 6.2 to 6.8, according to Wikipedia [^1].
- NPR reported that the alert system malfunctioned on the day [^2].
- The Uranus Building's partial collapse renewed scrutiny of older structures with weak ground floors [^1].
- Several of the deaths were caused by rockfalls on mountain trails and roads, highlighting the vulnerability of tourist sites in steep terrain [^4].
- Full restoration of Taroko National Park was not expected until around 2031, prolonging economic losses [^5].

## Recent News
Parts of Taroko National Park, including the Tianxiang area, reopened in stages beginning in late 2024, though officials said full repairs would take until about 2031 [^5]. No later official revision of the casualty figures was verified for this article.
