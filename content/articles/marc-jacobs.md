## Summary

Marc Jacobs (born April 9, 1963) is an American fashion designer best known as the founder of his namesake label and as the creative director of Louis Vuitton from 1997 to 2013. He is one of the most decorated designers in the history of the Council of Fashion Designers of America (CFDA), and his work has repeatedly redefined what American fashion could look like on a global stage.

Jacobs matters because he collapsed the distance between street culture, art, and luxury. His 1992 "grunge" collection for Perry Ellis got him fired but changed the industry's relationship to youth culture, and his artist collaborations at Louis Vuitton turned a heritage luggage house into a pop-culture phenomenon. Over four decades he has remained a fixture of New York Fashion Week and a reference point for younger designers.

## Early Life & Background

Jacobs was born in New York City. His father died when he was seven, and he was raised largely by his paternal grandmother on Manhattan's Upper West Side. He attended the High School of Art and Design and then Parsons School of Design, graduating in 1984. While still a student he designed hand-knit sweaters sold at the Charivari boutique, and at Parsons he received the Perry Ellis Gold Thimble Award, the Chester Weinberg Gold Thimble Award, and the Design Student of the Year award.

## Rise to Prominence

In 1984 Jacobs and business partner Robert Duffy produced their first collection under the Sketchbook label for Reuben Thomas Inc. In 1986 he showed his first collection under his own name, and in 1987 he became the youngest designer to receive the CFDA Perry Ellis Award for New Fashion Talent. In 1988 he was named vice president of women's design at Perry Ellis. His Spring 1993 grunge collection for the house, shown in late 1992, drew on flannel, thermals, and Doc Martens; Perry Ellis dismissed him, yet the CFDA named him Womenswear Designer of the Year for 1992.

Jacobs and Duffy founded Marc Jacobs International in 1993. In 1997 LVMH appointed him creative director of Louis Vuitton, where he launched the house's first ready-to-wear line, and the group took a stake in his own company the same year. The diffusion line Marc by Marc Jacobs launched in 2001 and was folded back into the main brand in 2015. He presented his final Louis Vuitton show in October 2013.

## Key Achievements

- **Louis Vuitton collaborations:** Stephen Sprouse graffiti bags (2001), Takashi Murakami's Multicolore monogram (2003), Richard Prince (2008), and Yayoi Kusama (2012) became some of the most commercially successful luxury products of their era.
- **CFDA honors:** Womenswear Designer of the Year in 1992, 1997, 1998, 2005, and 2010; Menswear Designer of the Year in 2002; Accessory Designer of the Year in 1998, 2003, and 2005; and the Geoffrey Beene Lifetime Achievement Award in 2011.
- **Fragrance and beauty:** The Daisy fragrance, launched in 2007, became a lasting bestseller, and Marc Jacobs Beauty launched with Sephora in 2013.
- **Recognition:** Named to the Time 100 in 2010 and appointed Chevalier of the Ordre des Arts et des Lettres by France the same year.
- **Heaven by Marc Jacobs**, launched in 2020, revived the brand's appeal with younger buyers through vintage-inspired pieces and subcultural collaborations.

## Controversies & Criticism

Jacobs' Spring 2017 show in September 2016 featured mostly white models wearing colorful dreadlocks, prompting accusations of cultural appropriation. His initial Instagram response, which compared the criticism to women of color straightening their hair, drew further backlash, and he later apologized. In December 2018 Nirvana LLC sued Marc Jacobs International over a smiley-face design in the "Bootleg Redux Grunge" collection, arguing it copied the band's logo. He has also been criticized for late runway starts, including a roughly 90-minute delay at his September 2018 show. Jacobs has spoken publicly about his treatment for addiction in 1999 and 2007, and the brand faced reported financial strain after Marc by Marc Jacobs was discontinued.

## Legacy & Influence

Jacobs helped establish the template for the modern creative director: a designer who steers a heritage house through art partnerships, celebrity casting, and theatrical shows. His grunge collection is now studied as a turning point in the acceptance of street style in high fashion, and his Louis Vuitton tenure demonstrated how collaboration could expand a luxury brand's audience. Designers including Nicolas Ghesquière, his successor at Vuitton, and a generation of New York labels cite his mix of irreverence and craft as a model.
