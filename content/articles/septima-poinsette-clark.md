## Summary

Septima Poinsette Clark (May 3, 1898 – December 15, 1987) was an American educator and civil rights activist whose Citizenship Schools taught literacy and civic knowledge to Black adults across the South so they could register to vote. Martin Luther King Jr. called her the "Mother of the Movement," and historians credit her program as the grassroots infrastructure beneath the voter-registration campaigns of the 1960s.

Her career spanned four decades as a classroom teacher in South Carolina, a decade at the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee, and nine years as director of education for the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC). She built a model in which ordinary community members, not credentialed teachers, taught their neighbors to read, write, and navigate the state's discriminatory voting laws.

## Early Life & Background

Clark was born in Charleston, South Carolina. Her father, Peter Poinsette, had been enslaved; her mother, Victoria Warren Anderson, was born free and raised in Haiti. She graduated from the Avery Normal Institute in 1916. Because Charleston barred Black teachers from its public schools, she took her first job at a two-room school on Johns Island, where she taught children by day and adults at night.

In 1919 she returned to Charleston to teach at Avery and joined the NAACP. She helped gather roughly 10,000 signatures in a petition campaign that led the state legislature in 1920 to permit Black teachers in Charleston's public schools. She married Nerie Clark in 1920; he died in 1925, leaving her with a son. She later earned a bachelor's degree from Benedict College (1942) and a master's from Hampton Institute (1946), and worked with Thurgood Marshall on the 1945 case that equalized Black and white teacher salaries in South Carolina.

## Rise to Prominence

In 1956 the South Carolina legislature prohibited state employees from belonging to the NAACP. Clark refused to resign her membership and was dismissed after 40 years of teaching, losing her pension. The state restored it, with back pay, in 1976.

That same year Myles Horton hired her as director of workshops at the Highlander Folk School in Monteagle, Tennessee, an integrated training center for labor and civil rights organizers. Rosa Parks had attended a Highlander workshop in the summer of 1955, months before the Montgomery bus boycott. In January 1957 Clark, Johns Island activist Esau Jenkins, and her cousin Bernice Robinson opened the first Citizenship School in the back room of a Johns Island cooperative store.

## Key Achievements

The Citizenship School curriculum used practical materials such as mail-order catalogs, voter-registration forms, and the state constitution. Students learned to sign their names, fill out forms, and answer the literacy tests used to disqualify Black voters. When Tennessee revoked Highlander's charter in 1961, the program moved to the SCLC, and Clark became its director of education and teaching.

By 1970, when she retired from the SCLC, the program had established close to 900 Citizenship Schools and trained thousands of local teachers who reached tens of thousands of students. Many graduates went on to lead local registration drives, and figures such as Fannie Lou Hamer passed through the program. King invited Clark to accompany him to Oslo for the 1964 Nobel Peace Prize ceremony.

Clark published two memoirs, *Echo in My Soul* (1962) and *Ready from Within* (1986), the latter of which won an American Book Award in 1987. She was elected to the Charleston County School Board in 1975, received the Living Legacy Award from President Jimmy Carter in 1979, and was awarded South Carolina's Order of the Palmetto in 1982.

## Controversies & Criticism

Clark was an open critic of sexism inside the movement she served. She later said the SCLC's male leadership "didn't have any faith in women," and she recounted that her suggestion that King share leadership rather than personally headline every march was laughed off at a staff meeting. Her insistence that the Citizenship Schools were the "base" of the movement conflicted with a leadership style built around charismatic figures.

Her association with Highlander drew accusations of Communist ties from segregationist officials, and a 1959 police raid on the school led to her arrest. The charges were later dropped.

## Legacy & Influence

The Citizenship School model influenced the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee's Freedom Schools and later adult-education programs. Charleston named a major expressway the Septima P. Clark Parkway, and her papers are held at the College of Charleston's Avery Research Center. She was inducted into the South Carolina Hall of Fame. Clark died on Johns Island on December 15, 1987, at age 89.
