## Summary

Oliver Reginald Tambo (27 October 1917 – 24 April 1993) was a South African anti-apartheid activist and the longest-serving president of the African National Congress (ANC), leading the organisation from 1967 to 1991. Working mostly from exile after 1960, he held the ANC together for three decades while its most prominent leaders, including Nelson Mandela, were imprisoned.

Tambo built the ANC's international diplomatic network, oversaw its armed wing Umkhonto we Sizwe (MK), and campaigned for the sanctions and boycotts that isolated the apartheid government. He returned to South Africa in December 1990 after 30 years abroad and died in 1993, a year before the country's first democratic election.

## Early Life & Background

Tambo was born on 27 October 1917 in the village of Nkantolo near Bizana in the Mpondoland region of the Eastern Cape. He attended mission schools, including Holy Cross Mission and St Peter's School in Johannesburg, where he matriculated with a first-class pass in 1938.

He enrolled at the University of Fort Hare, graduating with a Bachelor of Science degree in 1941. He was expelled in 1942 during a student strike while studying for a teaching diploma. He then returned to St Peter's to teach science and mathematics, where his pupils included future ANC leaders.

## Rise to Prominence

In 1944 Tambo, Nelson Mandela, Walter Sisulu and Anton Lembede co-founded the ANC Youth League, which pushed the organisation toward mass action. Tambo studied law and in 1952 opened Mandela & Tambo in Johannesburg, the first Black-owned law firm in South Africa.

He became ANC Secretary-General in 1955 and Deputy President in 1958. He was among the 156 accused in the 1956 Treason Trial, though charges against him were dropped in 1957. After the Sharpeville massacre in March 1960 and the banning of the ANC, the organisation sent Tambo abroad to build an external mission. He left South Africa in late March 1960 and did not return for 30 years.

## Key Achievements

Tambo became Acting President of the ANC after Chief Albert Luthuli's death in 1967 and was formally elected President in 1969 at the Morogoro Conference in Tanzania, which opened ANC membership to all races. He held the presidency until 1991.

From headquarters in London and Lusaka, he secured recognition for the ANC from the United Nations, the Organisation of African Unity and the Non-Aligned Movement, and opened missions in more than 25 countries. He personally lobbied governments to adopt sanctions, arms embargoes and cultural boycotts.

In 1985 he backed the Kabwe Conference decisions that intensified the armed struggle, and in 1987 and 1989 he directed contacts with South African business leaders and Afrikaner intellectuals that helped prepare the ground for negotiations. He was the principal author of the 1989 Harare Declaration, which set out the ANC's conditions for talks.

## Controversies & Criticism

Tambo's leadership coincided with the ANC's turn to armed struggle, and MK bombings such as the 1983 Church Street attack in Pretoria, which killed 19 people, drew criticism for civilian casualties. Tambo acknowledged that civilians had died and in 1980 committed the ANC to the Geneva Conventions.

He was also criticised for abuses in ANC detention camps in Angola, notably Quatro, where suspected spies and mutineers were tortured, and for the handling of the 1984 MK mutiny. The 1993 Motsuenyane Commission, established under the ANC, found that serious abuses had occurred.

## Legacy & Influence

Tambo suffered a stroke in August 1989 and handed the ANC presidency to Mandela in July 1991, becoming National Chairperson. He died of a stroke on 24 April 1993 at the age of 75 and was buried in Wattville, Benoni. In 2006 Johannesburg International Airport was renamed O. R. Tambo International Airport, and the Ekurhuleni district containing his home was named the O. R. Tambo District Municipality. South Africa awarded him the Order of the Companions of O. R. Tambo, a national honour named after him, and he was posthumously awarded the Isitwalandwe Medal, the ANC's highest honour, in 1992.
