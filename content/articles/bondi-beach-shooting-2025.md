## Summary

On the evening of 14 December 2025, two gunmen opened fire on a Chanukah by the Sea celebration in Archer Park at Bondi Beach, Sydney. About 1,000 people were attending the event, which marked the first night of Hanukkah [^1]. Fifteen people were killed, including a 10-year-old girl, and about 40 were injured [^1][^2]. Police shot dead one alleged attacker, 50-year-old Sajid Akram. His son, 24-year-old Naveed Akram, was wounded and charged with terrorism and 15 counts of murder [^2]. It was the deadliest mass shooting in Australia since Port Arthur in 1996, and the first deadly attack targeting Jews in the country. It led to new gun and hate crime laws and a royal commission [^1][^3].

## Background

Antisemitic incidents in Australia rose sharply after the Gaza war began in October 2023 [^1][^3]. In August 2024 ASIO raised the national terrorism threat level to "probable" [^1]. About 120,000 Jews live in Australia, less than 0.5 percent of the population [^3]. Although Australia tightened its gun laws after Port Arthur, gun licences in New South Wales rose from 181,000 in 2001 to 260,000 in 2025 [^1]. Sajid Akram held a firearms licence and six registered guns [^1].

## The Event

Police allege that the Akrams threw several improvised bombs from a footbridge over the car park north of the Bondi Pavilion, none of which exploded, and then fired into the crowd [^1]. The attack began at about 6:40 pm local time [^2]. Several bystanders tried to stop the gunmen. Boris and Sofia Gurman struggled with one attacker and were shot dead, and Reuven Morrison was killed after throwing a brick at a gunman [^1]. Ahmed al-Ahmed, a 43-year-old Syrian-born Muslim, tackled one gunman and wrestled his weapon away, and was then shot and wounded [^1]. Surf lifesavers treated victims under fire and sheltered about 250 people in their club [^1]. A police detective shot Sajid Akram dead, and Naveed was wounded and arrested. The shooting lasted about six minutes [^1]. Police found improvised explosive devices and homemade Islamic State flags in the attackers' car [^2].

## Response and Aftermath

Australian Federal Police Commissioner Krissy Barrett said early indications pointed to "a terrorist attack inspired by Islamic State" [^2]. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese called it "an act of pure evil, an act of antisemitism and terrorism" [^1]. On 17 December Naveed Akram was charged with 59 offences, including 15 counts of murder and one count of committing a terrorist act [^1][^2]. National Cabinet agreed to tighten gun laws, and Albanese announced a national gun buyback on 19 December [^1]. Parliament was recalled and passed gun control and anti-hate legislation on 20 January 2026 [^1]. On 8 January 2026 Albanese set up a Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion led by former High Court justice Virginia Bell [^1][^3]. A national day of mourning was held on 22 January [^1].

## Positive Perspectives

- Bystanders, including Ahmed al-Ahmed, Reuven Morrison, and the Gurmans, confronted the gunmen. Their actions drew praise from leaders around the world [^1].
- Surf lifesavers and first responders gave first aid under fire and sheltered hundreds of people [^1].
- Federal and state governments quickly agreed on tighter gun laws and a buyback, and NSW now limits most owners to four guns [^1][^6].
- The royal commission's interim report made 14 recommendations, including a review of joint counter-terrorism teams and stronger security at high-risk Jewish events [^4].

## Negative Perspectives

- The royal commission found that only three general-duties officers and a supervisor were assigned to the event. They were told there was "no need to stay the entire duration," even though a community security group had warned an attack was "likely" [^4].
- ASIO had examined Naveed Akram's associations in 2019 as part of an investigation into an Islamic State cell, which raised questions about missed warning signs [^1].
- The NSW Court of Appeal struck down protest restrictions passed after the attack, ruling them unconstitutional. The ruling cast doubt on charges against people arrested at a February 2026 protest [^1][^5].
- The Australian National Imams Council and the government's Islamophobia envoy reported a sharp rise in anti-Muslim incidents after the attack [^1].

## Recent News

The royal commission began public hearings on 24 February 2026 and released its interim report on 30 April 2026. The interim report found no gaps in the legal framework that would have prevented authorities from stopping the attack, but it said counter-terrorism capability could be improved [^3][^4]. Its final report is due before the first anniversary of the attack [^4]. On 10 June Naveed Akram was charged with 19 further offences, and his case is next listed for 28 October 2026 [^1]. NSW is due to begin its gun buyback on 2 November 2026, but a fully national scheme has stalled over funding disagreements [^6]. On 16 April 2026 the NSW Court of Appeal unanimously invalidated the post-attack protest laws [^1][^5].
