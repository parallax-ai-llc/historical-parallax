## Summary

Andrea Palladio (30 November 1508 – 19 August 1580) was an Italian Renaissance architect active in the Venetian Republic, widely regarded as one of the most influential figures in the history of Western architecture. Born Andrea di Pietro della Gondola in Padua, he trained as a stonemason before being taken up by the humanist Gian Giorgio Trissino, who renamed him "Palladio" after the Greek goddess Pallas Athena.

Palladio designed dozens of villas, palaces, and churches in and around Vicenza and Venice, applying proportional systems drawn from ancient Roman architecture and the writings of Vitruvius. His treatise *I quattro libri dell'architettura* (The Four Books of Architecture), published in Venice in 1570, spread his ideas across Europe and later to the Americas.

The style that bears his name, Palladianism, shaped British country houses in the 18th century and informed civic and domestic architecture in the United States, including Thomas Jefferson's Monticello. The City of Vicenza and the Palladian Villas of the Veneto were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994, expanded in 1996.

## Early Life & Background

Palladio was born in Padua, then part of the Republic of Venice, the son of Pietro della Gondola, a miller. At age 13 he was apprenticed to the stonecutter Bartolomeo Cavazza in Padua, but he broke the contract and moved to Vicenza in 1524, where he joined the guild of masons and stonecutters. He worked in the workshop of Giovanni di Giacomo da Porlezza and Girolamo Pittoni.

Around 1538 to 1539 he was working on the Villa Cricoli, owned by the poet and scholar Gian Giorgio Trissino. Trissino recognized his talent, educated him in classical literature and Vitruvian theory, and gave him the name Palladio. Trissino took him to Rome in 1541, the first of several study trips (further visits followed in 1545 to 1547 and 1554), during which Palladio measured and drew ancient ruins.

## Rise to Prominence

Palladio's first independent villa commission was the Villa Godi at Lonedo, begun around 1537 and completed in 1542. His breakthrough came in 1549, when the Vicenza city council chose his design to reconstruct the loggias of the Palazzo della Ragione, now known as the Basilica Palladiana. The project occupied him for the rest of his life and was finished only in 1614.

Through the 1540s and 1550s he built a series of palaces and villas for the Vicentine nobility, including the Palazzo Thiene (from 1542), Palazzo Chiericati (begun 1550), and Villa Barbaro at Maser (about 1554 to 1558), decorated with frescoes by Paolo Veronese. In 1554 he published a guidebook, *L'antichità di Roma*, and in 1556 he provided illustrations for Daniele Barbaro's edition of Vitruvius. After Jacopo Sansovino's death in 1570, Palladio became the leading architect of the Venetian state.

## Key Achievements

- **Villa Capra "La Rotonda"** (begun about 1567), near Vicenza, a centrally planned villa with four identical temple-front porticoes, his most imitated design.
- **San Giorgio Maggiore** (begun 1566) and **Il Redentore** (1577 to 1592) in Venice, which resolved the problem of applying a classical temple front to a basilica with a nave and lower aisles by overlapping two pediments.
- **The Four Books of Architecture** (1570), which combined practical building advice, reconstructions of Roman monuments, and illustrations of his own works. It was translated into English in 1715 to 1720 and became a standard reference for two centuries.
- **Teatro Olimpico** in Vicenza, begun in 1580 shortly before his death and completed by Vincenzo Scamozzi in 1585, the oldest surviving enclosed theatre in Europe.
- Twenty-three buildings in Vicenza and 24 villas in the Veneto are listed in the UNESCO World Heritage inscription.

## Controversies & Criticism

Palladio's reputation has generated relatively little personal controversy, but his work and legacy have attracted critical debate. Some art historians have argued that his published drawings idealized his buildings, presenting symmetrical plans and finished elevations that did not match what was actually constructed, as at the Palazzo Chiericati and Villa Barbaro. Scholars also disagree on how much the Villa Barbaro's design owes to its patron, Daniele Barbaro.

The later Palladian movement has been criticized for turning his flexible principles into rigid formulae. Critics of 18th-century Palladianism, and of 20th-century neoclassical revivals, have described the style as conservative or as an expression of elite taste. Attribution problems persist too, since many minor Veneto villas are assigned to Palladio on stylistic grounds alone.

## Legacy & Influence

Palladio is the only architect to have an entire style named after him. Inigo Jones brought his ideas to England after visiting the Veneto in 1613 to 1614, and the Queen's House at Greenwich (1616 to 1635) launched English Palladianism. The 1715 publication of Colen Campbell's *Vitruvius Britannicus* and Lord Burlington's Chiswick House (about 1729) made the style dominant in Britain for decades.

In North America, Palladian principles shaped Drayton Hall in South Carolina (1738 to 1742), Jefferson's Monticello and the University of Virginia, and the Palladian window became a fixture of Georgian and Federal architecture. In 2010, the United States Congress passed a resolution recognizing his 500th birthday and calling him the "Father of American Architecture." The Centro Internazionale di Studi di Architettura Andrea Palladio, founded in Vicenza in 1958, continues research into his work.
