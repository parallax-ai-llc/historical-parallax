## Summary

At 12:33 CEST on 28 April 2025, the electricity grids of mainland Spain and Portugal collapsed, cutting power to tens of millions of people and disconnecting about 31 GW of load [^1]. Andorra and parts of southwestern France were briefly affected [^1]. Power was restored to 99 percent of Spain by the next morning, but eight deaths were linked to the outage [^1]. European grid experts later called it the most severe blackout in Europe in more than 20 years and the first known to be caused by overvoltage [^2].

## Background

By 2025 the Iberian Peninsula generated a large share of its electricity from renewable sources and had relatively weak interconnections with the rest of Europe [^1]. At the time of the blackout, solar power supplied about 59 percent of Spanish demand, largely through grid-following inverters that provide little voltage support, and relatively few conventional thermal plants were running to control voltage [^1]. Investigators also identified low-frequency oscillations on the grid in the minutes before the collapse [^1][^2].

## The Event

From about 12:32 p.m. an uncontrolled rise in voltage triggered a chain of generator disconnections in southern Spain [^2]. As more plants tripped, voltage rose further, and the Iberian grid lost synchronization with the continental European system [^2]. Metro systems, trains, traffic lights, telecommunications and payment systems stopped across both countries [^1]. Seven deaths in Spain and one in Portugal were attributed to secondary causes such as carbon monoxide poisoning from generators, failure of home oxygen equipment, and candle fires [^1]. Grid operators restored power over the following hours using hydroelectric plants and interconnections with France and Morocco, and Portugal was fully restored early on 29 April [^1].

## Response and Aftermath

On 17 June 2025 Spain's government approved a report presented by Ecological Transition Minister Sara Aagesen that ruled out a cyberattack and described the blackout as "multifactorial" [^3]. The government's report criticized grid operator Red Electrica for poor planning and also pointed to generators that may not have complied with voltage-control obligations [^3]. Red Electrica, in turn, argued that conventional plants had failed to control voltage as required [^1]. Spain approved Royal Decree-Law 7/2025 on grid storage and operating procedures, which was later repealed, while Portugal announced about 400 million euros of grid investment [^1]. The expert panel of the European Network of Transmission System Operators for Electricity (ENTSO-E) published its final report in March 2026 [^2].

## Positive Perspectives

- Operators in both countries restored nearly all power within about a day, without lasting damage to the grid [^1].
- The official investigation ruled out a cyberattack, easing fears of hostile interference [^3].
- Portugal committed about 400 million euros to expand black-start capability and to increase battery storage from under 20 MW to 750 MW [^1].
- ENTSO-E's final report recommended stronger operating practices, better monitoring and closer data sharing among grid participants [^2].

## Negative Perspectives

- Spain's government and Red Electrica publicly blamed each other and the power companies, and the operator complained that utilities had not provided complete information [^3][^4].
- The final ENTSO-E report found gaps in voltage and reactive power control and uneven stabilization capabilities across the system [^2].
- About 35,000 passengers had to be rescued from stranded trains and metro systems, and hospitals were forced onto backup generators [^1].
- The blackout fuelled a political debate over renewables and nuclear power, although officials and the expert panel said the problem was voltage control rather than renewable energy itself [^1].
- Spain's emergency decree-law intended to strengthen the grid was later repealed [^1].

## Recent News

ENTSO-E's expert panel released its final report on 20 March 2026, concluding that the blackout resulted from many interacting factors rather than a single cause [^2][^5].
