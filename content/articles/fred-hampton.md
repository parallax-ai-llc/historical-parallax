## Summary

Fred Hampton (August 30, 1948 – December 4, 1969) was an American activist and chairman of the Illinois chapter of the Black Panther Party. In little more than a year of leadership he built one of the party's most effective chapters, launched community programs such as a free breakfast program and a medical clinic, and negotiated a multiracial "Rainbow Coalition" of Chicago street organizations.

He was killed at age 21 during a predawn raid on his apartment by Chicago police officers working with the Cook County State's Attorney's Office. Later investigations showed that the FBI's COINTELPRO program had supplied a floor plan of the apartment and that police fired nearly all of the shots. His death became a defining example of state repression of Black political movements, and a 1982 civil settlement of $1.85 million was paid to the survivors and the families of Hampton and Mark Clark.

## Early Life & Background

Hampton was born in Summit, Illinois, a suburb of Chicago, and grew up in nearby Maywood. His parents, Francis and Iberia Hampton, had moved north from Louisiana and worked at the Argo Starch plant. He graduated from Proviso East High School in 1966, where he was an honor student and athlete, and then studied pre-law at Triton Junior College.

While still a teenager he led the youth council of the West Suburban branch of the NAACP, growing its membership to several hundred and campaigning for a community swimming pool and better recreational facilities for Black residents of Maywood.

## Rise to Prominence

In November 1968 Hampton joined the newly formed Illinois chapter of the Black Panther Party and quickly became its chairman. He was known as a forceful public speaker who could hold crowds for hours, and he shifted the chapter's emphasis toward "survival programs" that served the community directly.

Under his leadership the Chicago chapter opened a free breakfast program that fed hundreds of children each week, established a free medical clinic, ran political education classes, and organized community policing patrols. In April 1969 he announced the Rainbow Coalition, an alliance between the Panthers, the Young Lords, and the Young Patriots Organization that brought together Black, Puerto Rican, and poor white Chicagoans.

## Key Achievements

- **Rainbow Coalition (1969):** Brokered a truce among rival groups and formed a class-based, multiracial political alliance that inspired later coalitions.
- **Survival programs:** Built a free breakfast program, a medical clinic, and educational classes serving Chicago's West Side.
- **Party leadership:** Was slated to become chief of staff of the national Black Panther Party and a member of its Central Committee shortly before his death.

## Controversies & Criticism

In 1969 Hampton was convicted of stealing $71 worth of ice cream bars and distributing them to children in Maywood. He received a sentence of two to five years and was released on appeal bond in August 1969.

Critics of the Black Panther Party, including FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover, characterized the group as a violent threat, and Hampton's rhetoric about armed self-defense drew condemnation. Supporters countered that the party's programs were overwhelmingly community-focused.

The circumstances of his death remain the central controversy. William O'Neal, an FBI informant serving as chapter security chief, provided the apartment's layout to the bureau. In the raid of December 4, 1969, police fired between 90 and 99 shots while the occupants fired one. Hampton, who forensic evidence suggested had been drugged, was shot twice in the head. A federal grand jury in 1970 found the police account inconsistent with physical evidence, yet no officer was ever convicted.

## Legacy & Influence

Hampton's death spurred wider scrutiny of COINTELPRO, contributing to the 1976 Church Committee findings on domestic surveillance abuses. Chicago named December 4 "Fred Hampton Day" in 1990, and his childhood home in Maywood has been the subject of preservation efforts.

His life and killing have been depicted in the documentary *The Murder of Fred Hampton* (1971) and the film *Judas and the Black Messiah* (2021), for which Daniel Kaluuya won an Academy Award for portraying him. His son, Fred Hampton Jr., continues activist work in Chicago.
