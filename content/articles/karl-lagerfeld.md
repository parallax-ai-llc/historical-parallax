## Summary

Karl Lagerfeld (10 September 1933 – 19 February 2019) was a German fashion designer, creative director and photographer who led Chanel for 36 years, Fendi for 54 years, and his own label for 35 years, often simultaneously. His white ponytail, dark glasses, high starched collars and fingerless gloves made him one of the most recognizable figures in twentieth- and twenty-first-century fashion.

He is credited with reviving Chanel after founder Gabrielle Chanel's death, turning a house widely seen as dated in 1983 into one of the world's most valuable luxury brands. At Fendi he transformed a Roman fur and leather-goods firm into a global ready-to-wear name and designed its double-F logo.

Lagerfeld's output was unusually prolific: he was reported to produce as many as 14 collections a year across his houses while also shooting many of their advertising campaigns himself.

## Early Life & Background

Lagerfeld was born in Hamburg to Otto Lagerfeld, a businessman who introduced Glücksklee condensed milk to Germany, and Elisabeth Bahlmann. He frequently gave his birth year as 1938, but Hamburg baptismal records and a 2008 newspaper investigation established 1933. The family spent much of the Second World War on an estate near Bad Bramstedt in Schleswig-Holstein.

He moved to Paris in 1952 to finish his schooling and study drawing. In 1954 he entered the International Wool Secretariat design competition and won the coat category; Yves Saint Laurent won the dress category the same year.

## Rise to Prominence

Pierre Balmain, a competition judge, hired Lagerfeld as an assistant in 1955. He became artistic director at Jean Patou in 1958 and left in 1962 to work freelance, an unusual choice at the time. From 1964 he designed for Chloé, becoming its sole designer in 1974 and returning for a second stint from 1992 to 1997. Fendi engaged him in 1965, a relationship that continued until his death.

In 1983 Chanel's owner Alain Wertheimer appointed him artistic director. Lagerfeld reworked the house codes of tweed, chains, pearls and camellias with irony and street references, and expanded Chanel's ready-to-wear and accessories. He launched the Karl Lagerfeld label in 1984.

## Key Achievements

- Chanel creative director, 1983–2019, overseeing haute couture, ready-to-wear and the Métiers d'art collections launched in 2002.
- Creative director of fur and ready-to-wear at Fendi, 1965–2019.
- Designed the H&M collaboration of November 2004, which sold out within hours and established the designer-mass-market model later used by Stella McCartney, Versace and Balmain.
- Took up photography in 1987, shooting Chanel campaigns and editorials for Vogue, Harper's Bazaar and Numéro, and publishing photo books through Steidl.
- Staged large-scale Chanel shows at the Grand Palais in Paris from 2005, including a supermarket (2014), an airport terminal (2016) and a rocket launch (2017).
- Lost 42 kilograms in 2001 and co-wrote The Karl Lagerfeld Diet (2002) with Jean-Claude Houdret.

## Controversies & Criticism

Lagerfeld regularly drew criticism for public remarks. In 2012 he called singer Adele "a little too fat," later apologizing. In 2013 he said no one wanted to see "curvy women" on runways, and in 2009 he dismissed critics of thin models. In November 2017 on French television he criticized Angela Merkel's refugee policy with a comparison involving the Holocaust, prompting complaints to the broadcasting regulator. In 2018 he told Numéro he was "fed up" with the #MeToo movement and questioned models' harassment complaints. Animal rights groups repeatedly protested his fur work at Fendi.

## Legacy & Influence

Lagerfeld died in Neuilly-sur-Seine on 19 February 2019 from complications of pancreatic cancer. Virginie Viard, his long-time studio director, succeeded him at Chanel; Silvia Venturini Fendi took over at Fendi. A memorial, "Karl For Ever," was held at the Grand Palais on 20 June 2019. The Metropolitan Museum of Art's 2023 Costume Institute exhibition, "Karl Lagerfeld: A Line of Beauty," presented roughly 150 of his designs and was the theme of that year's Met Gala. His model of the creative director as brand curator, reinterpreting a founder's codes season after season, remains the dominant template in luxury fashion.
