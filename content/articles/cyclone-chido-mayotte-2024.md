## Summary
Intense Tropical Cyclone Chido struck the French overseas department of Mayotte on 14 December 2024, the most powerful storm to hit the archipelago in at least 90 years, before making landfall in Mozambique and moving into Malawi [^1]. At least 173 deaths were attributed to the storm, including 40 in Mayotte, 120 in Mozambique and 13 in Malawi, but the Mayotte toll was heavily debated because many residents of destroyed shanty towns were unaccounted for [^1]. With losses of about $3.9 billion, Chido is the costliest cyclone on record in the South-West Indian Ocean [^1]. The disaster exposed deep poverty, informal housing and migration tensions in France's poorest department [^1][^4].

## Background
Mayotte, a French department in the Comoros archipelago, has a large population living in informal housing; AFP reported that shanty towns housed an estimated 100,000 of the 300,000 officially registered inhabitants [^2]. About a third of residents are undocumented migrants, mostly from the Comoros [^1]. Only about 10 percent of inhabitants had insurance [^2]. Chido began as a disturbance on 5 December 2024 and rapidly intensified on 11 December, reaching an estimated 1-minute sustained wind of 135 knots according to the US Joint Typhoon Warning Center [^1].

## The Event
Chido passed north of Madagascar and struck Mayotte on 14 December 2024, with winds of 226 km/h recorded at Dzaoudzi-Pamandzi airport, which was badly damaged [^1]. In the capital Mamoudzou most homes and administrative buildings were severely damaged, and entire shanty towns were destroyed, including much of Kaweni, the largest with about 20,000 residents [^1]. Some residents avoided shelters for fear of looting or deportation [^1]. By 16 December, 85 percent of Mayotte's households were still without power, and a nighttime curfew began on 17 December [^1]. Chido made landfall near Pemba, Mozambique, on 15 December, damaging more than 155,500 homes there, before weakening over Mozambique and Malawi on 16 December [^1].

## Response and Aftermath
Prefect Francois-Xavier Bieuville called Chido the most violent and destructive cyclone since 1934 and said it would be "extremely hard" to count the dead [^1]. Interior Minister Bruno Retailleau visited on 16 December, and President Emmanuel Macron declared national mourning for 23 December [^1]. During a visit on 19 December, Macron was jeered by residents demanding water and told them that "if this wasn't France, you'd be 10,000 times more in the shit," a remark that drew criticism [^1][^3]. Parliament passed an emergency reconstruction law, promulgated on 25 February 2025, offering zero-interest home loans of up to €50,000 and relaxing planning rules [^5]. A broader refoundation law, published on 12 August 2025, set out nearly €4 billion of investment over six years [^6].

## Positive Perspectives
- The state deployed about 4,000 personnel and distributed humanitarian aid quickly after the storm [^4].
- Residents organised to clear roads and debris within days, according to AFP reporting from Pamandzi [^2].
- The emergency law reserved up to 30 percent of contract values for local small businesses and raised tax deductions for donations [^5].
- The refoundation law included spending on water, sanitation, health, education and housing, with a monitoring committee reporting to the prime minister [^6].

## Negative Perspectives
- The official Mayotte death toll was widely questioned, as many residents of destroyed shanty towns were unaccounted for [^1].
- Little was done to prepare Mayotte for the storm, according to the Wikipedia account [^1].
- Residents still lacking food, water and electricity met Macron's remark with jeers and calls for his resignation [^3].
- A University of Montpellier geographer said only about €25 million, or 0.6 percent of the promised budget, had been spent by December 2025 [^4].
- Residents without legal status cannot access public housing, so informal settlements were rebuilt in the same vulnerable form, and the geographer described deportation efforts as ineffective amid migration tensions [^4].

## Recent News
At the first anniversary in December 2025, a University of Montpellier geographer said schools, water supply and housing remained badly affected, that promised zero-interest loans were largely out of reach, and that shanty towns had been rebuilt from salvaged materials [^4].
