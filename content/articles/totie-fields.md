## Summary

Totie Fields (born Sophie Feldman; May 7, 1930 – August 2, 1978) was an American stand-up comedian who became one of the most recognizable female comics of the 1960s and 1970s. Standing under five feet tall and built her act around self-deprecating jokes about her weight, her marriage, and her Jewish upbringing, she rose from the Catskills resort circuit to headline Las Vegas showrooms and appear repeatedly on the era's dominant television variety programs.

Fields mattered because she carved out a headlining career in nightclub comedy at a time when almost no women did so, and because of the public resilience she showed late in life. After losing a leg in 1976, she returned to the stage within months, a comeback that won her the American Guild of Variety Artists' Entertainer of the Year honor shortly before her death at 48.

## Early Life & Background

Fields was born Sophie Feldman in Hartford, Connecticut, on May 7, 1930, the youngest of four children in a Jewish family. Her stage name came from her own childhood mispronunciation of "Sophie" as "Totie," which stuck as a family nickname. Her mother died when she was a small child, and she was largely raised by her father and older siblings.

She began performing as a singer in her teens, working small clubs in the Boston area, and gradually shifted toward comedy after discovering that audiences responded more to her between-song patter than to her singing. In 1950 she married George Johnston, a musician who later became her manager. The couple had two daughters, Debby and Jody.

## Rise to Prominence

Fields spent the 1950s working the "Borscht Belt" hotels of New York's Catskill Mountains, the proving ground for many Jewish comedians of the period. She developed a brash, rapid-fire style in which her own body, appetite, and marriage were the primary targets. A 1963 engagement at New York's Copacabana nightclub brought her to the attention of Ed Sullivan, who booked her on his CBS program. Her appearances on *The Ed Sullivan Show* through the 1960s introduced her to a national audience.

From there she became a regular on talk and variety shows, including *The Tonight Show Starring Johnny Carson*, *The Mike Douglas Show*, and *The Merv Griffin Show*, and moved into the Las Vegas market, where she headlined at the Sahara and other Strip hotels. In 1972 she published a comic diet book, *I Think I'll Start on Monday: The Official 8½ oz. Mashed Potato Diet*.

## Key Achievements

- Became one of the first women to headline major Las Vegas showrooms as a solo stand-up comedian.
- Appeared on *The Ed Sullivan Show* repeatedly across the 1960s and was a frequent guest on the leading talk shows of the 1970s.
- Returned to performing in 1977, less than a year after her left leg was amputated above the knee following complications from phlebitis in 1976, working in a wheelchair and later with a prosthesis.
- Named Entertainer of the Year by the American Guild of Variety Artists in 1978.

## Controversies & Criticism

Fields generated little public controversy in her lifetime, but her act drew criticism on two fronts. Some contemporaries and later commentators argued that her reliance on jokes about her own weight reinforced the idea that a woman had to demean herself to be accepted as funny. Others considered her material broad and formulaic compared with the more observational comedy emerging in the 1970s. Her defenders countered that she controlled the joke rather than being its victim, and that her frankness about her body and later her disability was ahead of its time.

## Legacy & Influence

Fields died in Las Vegas on August 2, 1978, of a pulmonary embolism, months after undergoing a mastectomy for breast cancer. Her willingness to joke openly about amputation and illness on national television was widely noted at the time and has since been cited as an early example of comedians addressing disability directly. Female comedians of the following generations, including Joan Rivers and Roseanne Barr, worked in a self-deprecating, confessional mode that Fields helped establish. She remains a reference point in histories of women in American stand-up.
