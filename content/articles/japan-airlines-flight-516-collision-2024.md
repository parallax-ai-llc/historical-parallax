## Summary
At about 17:47 JST on 2 January 2024, Japan Airlines Flight 516, an Airbus A350-900 landing at Tokyo's Haneda Airport, struck a Japan Coast Guard Dash 8 aircraft that had entered runway 34R [^1]. Both aircraft burned; five of the six Coast Guard crew were killed, while all 379 people on the JAL jet escaped [^1][^2]. The Coast Guard plane had been preparing to fly relief supplies to areas hit by the Noto Peninsula earthquake the day before [^1]. The evacuation of the burning airliner became a widely cited example of cabin safety, while the collision itself exposed weaknesses in air traffic control communication [^2].

## Background
Haneda is one of the world's busiest airports, and on 2 January 2024 it was handling holiday traffic as well as relief flights after the 1 January Noto earthquake [^1]. The Coast Guard aircraft, a De Havilland Canada DHC-8-315 (JA722A), was due to carry emergency supplies to Niigata [^1]. JAL 516, an A350-941 (JA13XJ), was arriving from New Chitose Airport near Sapporo with 367 passengers and 12 crew [^1].

## The Event
Air traffic control cleared JAL 516 to land on runway 34R and instructed the Coast Guard aircraft to taxi to holding point C5, short of the runway, telling it that it was "number one" in the departure sequence [^1]. The Japan Transport Safety Board's interim report found that both the Coast Guard captain and co-pilot understood this as permission to enter the runway, and the aircraft lined up and stopped on it [^3]. The JAL crew did not see the small aircraft until just before impact, and the controller did not notice it on the runway; a runway occupancy alert displayed for roughly a minute went unnoticed [^2][^3]. The A350 hit the Dash 8 and slid down the runway in flames [^1]. Five Coast Guard crew died; the captain, Genki Miyamoto, escaped with serious injuries [^1]. Passengers and crew left the airliner down emergency slides, and the evacuation was completed by 17:58, about 11 minutes after the collision [^1][^4].

## Response and Aftermath
The JTSB opened a safety investigation, and Tokyo police began a separate inquiry into possible professional negligence [^2][^4]. The airport kept three of its four runways open, and runway 34R reopened about a week later; the disruption caused more than 1,200 flight cancellations, according to Outlook India [^5]. Japan's transport ministry stopped the use of "number one" and similar sequence phrases in departure instructions and moved to make stop lines at runway entrances more visible [^1]. On 25 December 2024 the JTSB published an interim report attributing the accident to a chain of human errors by the Coast Guard crew and air traffic control, with limited visibility from the JAL cockpit [^3][^2].

## Positive Perspectives
- All 379 occupants of JAL 516 survived a fire that destroyed the aircraft, an outcome widely credited to cabin crew performance and passenger compliance [^1][^2].
- Haneda kept three of its four runways operating and restored the damaged runway within about a week [^5].
- Authorities quickly changed controller phraseology, removing the "number one" sequencing language implicated in the misunderstanding [^1].
- The JTSB released detailed interim findings within a year and pledged recurrence-prevention measures in its final report [^3].

## Negative Perspectives
- The interim report found the controller missed the Coast Guard aircraft on the runway for about a minute despite an on-screen alert [^2][^3].
- Both Coast Guard pilots misread a clearance, raising questions about crew resource management and communication standards [^3].
- Five Coast Guard personnel on a humanitarian mission were killed [^1].
- AvWeb, summarizing the report, noted that the JAL crew could not identify the small aircraft because of limited sight lines [^2].
- A parallel police negligence investigation created tension with the safety-investigation principle of not apportioning blame [^4].

## Recent News
The JTSB's interim report of December 2024 remains the most detailed official account located in this research; a final report had been promised, but its publication could not be verified as of October 2026 [^3].
