## Summary

On 28 December 2025, shopkeepers in Tehran closed their businesses in protest against a record collapse of the rial. The protests soon spread nationwide and turned against the Islamic Republic itself [^1][^2]. After the authorities cut off the internet on 8 January 2026, security forces fired on crowds across the country. The UN fact-finding mission on Iran later found that this amounted to crimes against humanity [^1][^5][^6]. The death toll is deeply disputed. The government acknowledged 3,117 deaths, the rights group HRANA had documented more than 7,000 by February, and Iranian health officials quoted by Time estimated that as many as 30,000 were killed on 8 and 9 January alone [^1][^3][^4]. Wikipedia describes the movement as the largest uprising in Iran since the 1979 revolution [^1].

## Background

Iran's economy deteriorated sharply in 2025. The country faced energy shortages, damage from the June 2025 war with Israel and the United States, and UN "snapback" sanctions reimposed in September [^1]. By late December the rial had fallen to about 1.42 million to the US dollar. The official inflation rate was about 42 percent, with food prices up 72 percent year on year [^2]. Central bank governor Mohammad Reza Farzin resigned on 29 December [^2]. Protesters also denounced corruption and the government's spending on regional allies such as Hezbollah [^1].

## The Event

Merchants near Tehran's Grand Bazaar and on Saadi Street shut their shops on 28 December, and police used tear gas on demonstrators. Similar protests were reported in Isfahan, Shiraz, and Mashhad [^2]. University students joined, and slogans such as "Death to the Dictator" were aimed at Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei [^1]. Exiled opposition figure Reza Pahlavi called for coordinated protests on 8 January. That day the government imposed a near-total internet and telephone blackout [^1][^5]. By 9 January, protests had reached all 31 provinces [^1]. Hospitals in Tehran and Shiraz were overwhelmed with gunshot victims, and Amnesty International reported security forces firing rifles and metal pellets, often at people's heads and torsos [^1][^3]. HRANA reported more than 22,000 arrests by mid-January and 53,552 by mid-February [^1].

## Response and Aftermath

Iranian authorities called the unrest "terrorist" activity backed by the United States and Israel [^3]. Judiciary chief Gholamhossein Mohseni Ejei ordered prosecutors to show "no leniency," and death sentences followed [^1]. On 21 January state media gave the first official toll of 3,117 deaths [^3]. On 23 January the UN Human Rights Council held a special session and adopted a resolution condemning the crackdown, with 25 votes in favour, 7 against, and 14 abstentions. The resolution extended the mandate of the fact-finding mission for two years [^5]. US President Donald Trump threatened intervention. The crackdown was among the justifications the US later cited for the 2026 war on Iran [^1].

## Positive Perspectives

- Protesters from many ethnic and religious groups and social classes, including bazaar merchants, students, and workers, joined a nationwide movement across all 31 provinces [^1][^5].
- Rights groups including HRANA documented deaths and arrests case by case despite the blackout [^1][^4].
- The UN Human Rights Council strengthened international scrutiny by extending the fact-finding mission and the special rapporteur's mandate [^5].
- On 11 February President Masoud Pezeshkian publicly apologised to the nation for the killings [^1].

## Negative Perspectives

- The killings may be the deadliest crackdown in Iran since 1979. The fact-finding mission's chair used that description, and a former UN prosecutor called it "the worst mass murder in the contemporary history of Iran" [^5].
- Casualty figures cannot be independently verified. Estimates range from the official 3,117 to Time's report, sourced to health officials, of up to 30,000 deaths in two days [^3][^4].
- Rights groups reported executions of protesters, forced confessions, and torture. Amnesty International said in May that at least 78 people were under sentence of death [^1].
- The government blamed foreign enemies and cut off communications instead of addressing the economic grievances behind the protests [^1][^3].

## Recent News

Student protests resumed on 21 February 2026, and rooftop chanting continued during the 2026 war that began on 28 February [^1]. On 17 September 2026 the UN fact-finding mission reported that Iranian authorities had committed crimes against humanity in the crackdown. It cited killings "on a staggering scale," torture, and arbitrary detention, and said more than 70 people faced imminent execution [^6].
