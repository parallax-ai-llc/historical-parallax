## Summary
On the afternoon of 1 January 2024, a magnitude 7.6 (JMA scale; Mw 7.5) earthquake struck the Noto Peninsula in Ishikawa Prefecture, Japan, reaching the maximum intensity of shindo 7 [^1]. The quake, tsunami, and a large fire in Wajima destroyed or damaged tens of thousands of buildings in an aging, remote region [^1]. By late 2025 the Ishikawa prefectural government counted 698 deaths, of which 228 were direct and 470 were "disaster-related" deaths during evacuation, making it one of Japan's deadliest earthquakes since 2011 [^2][^3]. The slow pace of recovery has made Noto a reference point in Japanese debates about disaster response in depopulating rural areas [^3].

## Background
The Noto Peninsula juts into the Sea of Japan and had experienced an intense earthquake swarm for several years before 2024 [^1]. The region is sparsely populated, elderly, and connected to the rest of Honshu by a limited number of roads, factors that later complicated relief operations [^1][^3]. The Shika Nuclear Power Plant, offline at the time, sits on the peninsula's western coast [^1].

## The Event
The mainshock struck at 16:10 JST on New Year's Day, when many families had gathered at home for the holiday [^1]. Ground shaking reached shindo 7, the top of Japan's intensity scale, and a tsunami followed; Wikipedia, citing post-event surveys, reports a run-up of 11.3 m measured in Wajima [^1]. A fire broke out in central Wajima and consumed an estimated 200 buildings, including the historic Asaichi morning market district [^1]. Across nine prefectures more than 200,000 structures were reported damaged, with landslides and road collapses cutting off many coastal villages [^1]. More than 1,400 people were injured [^1].

## Response and Aftermath
The national government and the Self-Defense Forces mounted search and rescue operations, while Prime Minister Fumio Kishida's government released reserve funds for relief [^1]. Hundreds of residents in isolated settlements had to be evacuated by helicopter because roads were impassable [^1]. At the Shika plant, transformer damage and minor equipment problems were reported, though no radiation release occurred [^1]. In September 2024, torrential rain triggered floods and landslides in the same area, killing further residents; Jiji Press reported 20 deaths from that rain disaster [^2]. Clean-up was enormous: Ishikawa faced roughly 2.4 million tonnes of debris, by Wikipedia's account [^1].

## Positive Perspectives
- Residents of villages cut off by landslides and road collapses were evacuated by helicopter [^1].
- The Self-Defense Forces and other agencies carried out large-scale air and ground rescue in difficult terrain [^1].
- Government and prefectural authorities built thousands of temporary housing units; the number of residents in them peaked at about 22,000 [^3].
- Infrastructure repair advanced, and most destroyed homes had been demolished by the second anniversary, according to Jiji Press [^3].
- Annual memorials attended by national and prefectural leaders have kept attention on the recovery [^2].

## Negative Perspectives
- Wikipedia's account notes criticism that the government's initial response was slow and that early restrictions on volunteers hampered relief [^1].
- Disaster-related deaths, attributed to the physical and mental strain of evacuation and shelter life, have exceeded direct deaths by a wide margin [^2][^3].
- More than 18,000 people remained in temporary housing at the end of 2025, and construction of permanent public housing had only just begun in some towns [^3].
- The population of four Okunoto coastal municipalities fell by about 13 percent after the quake, raising doubts about long-term viability, according to Jiji Press [^3].
- Damage at the Shika nuclear plant renewed questions about nuclear safety in an active seismic zone [^1].
- The September 2024 floods struck communities still recovering, compounding losses [^2].

## Recent News
At the second anniversary on 1 January 2026, Jiji Press reported a prefectural toll of 698 deaths including indirect deaths, with two people still missing [^2]. Wikipedia lists a later figure of 732 deaths (228 direct and 504 disaster-related), reflecting continued certification of indirect deaths; totals differ by date and source [^1]. As of 1 December 2025, 18,586 people were still living in temporary housing [^3].
