## Summary
On 6 August 2024 the Armed Forces of Ukraine launched a surprise cross-border offensive into Russia's Kursk Oblast, the largest attack on Russian territory since the 2022 full-scale invasion and the first carried out mainly by Ukrainian regular forces [^1]. Ukraine said it held about 1,250 square kilometres and 92 settlements at the high point in August 2024 [^1]. A Russian counteroffensive, later supported by North Korean troops, recaptured the town of Sudzha in March 2025, and Moscow declared the region fully retaken on 26 April 2025, a claim Kyiv disputed [^1][^2]. The operation remains debated as either a bold strategic gamble or a costly diversion from Ukraine's eastern front [^1].

## Background
By mid-2024 Russian forces held the initiative in eastern Ukraine and were pressing toward Pokrovsk in Donetsk Oblast [^1]. Earlier raids into Russia had been carried out by pro-Ukrainian Russian volunteer groups that Kyiv supported while denying direct involvement [^1]. Ukrainian officials later said the Kursk operation was intended to damage Russia's military, capture prisoners for exchanges, push Russian artillery out of range, disrupt supply lines, divert Russian forces from other fronts, and pressure Moscow toward "fair" negotiations [^1].

## The Event
Ukrainian units crossed the border on 6 August 2024 and clashed with Russian army and border guard forces; Russia declared a state of emergency in Kursk Oblast and rushed reserves to the area [^1]. By the end of the first week the Ukrainian military claimed about 1,000 square kilometres, while Russian authorities acknowledged the loss of 28 settlements [^1]. Ukrainian forces took Sudzha, the area's largest town and the site of a gas metering hub on the Urengoy-Pomary-Uzhhorod pipeline [^1]. On 19 August President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Ukraine controlled 92 settlements and 1,250 square kilometres [^1].

The front stabilised in the second half of August, and the Ukrainian advance stalled by early October [^1]. From November 2024 North Korean troops were deployed to the province; Western estimates put the contingent at around 12,000 soldiers [^1][^3]. By the end of November Russia had recaptured roughly half of the territory Ukraine had taken [^1]. A Russian counterattack in March 2025 collapsed much of the salient: most Ukrainian forces had withdrawn by 11 March, and Russian troops entered Sudzha the following day [^1]. On 26 April 2025 Chief of the General Staff Valery Gerasimov told President Vladimir Putin that the last occupied village, Gornal, had been taken, and for the first time officially confirmed that North Korean soldiers had fought for Russia [^2]. Ukraine's General Staff called the announcement a "propaganda ploy" and said fighting continued [^2].

## Response and Aftermath
Russian authorities reported evacuating more than 144,000 civilians from Kursk and Belgorod oblasts, and Russia stated that 358 civilians were killed and 789 went missing; these figures have not been independently verified [^1]. Casualty claims are contested: Russia claimed more than 74,000 Ukrainian casualties, while Ukraine claimed about 77,000 Russian killed or wounded and 2,251 North Korean dead [^1]. Putin visited the region in May 2025, approved continued payments to displaced families, and backed a museum honouring Russian defenders [^3]. Even after Moscow's declaration, Ukraine's General Staff said its operations in "designated areas" of the border continued [^3].

## Positive Perspectives
- Supporters noted the operation achieved strategic surprise and was the first occupation of Russian territory by a foreign army since the Second World War [^3].
- Former military adviser Nico Lange assessed the offensive as a success [^1].
- Land warfare expert Nick Reynolds said Ukraine held the Sudzha pocket for a "remarkable" length of time and that the offensive had a "shaping effect on Russian thinking" [^1].
- The operation forced Moscow to commit reserves and to seek North Korean manpower, revealing limits on Russian forces [^1][^2].
- Ukraine said it captured Russian troops, which officials described as a goal tied to prisoner exchanges [^1].

## Negative Perspectives
- By late August 2024 critics argued the operation drained Ukrainian forces from the east and allowed Russia to advance toward Pokrovsk [^1].
- The Institute for the Study of War reported that Russia drew forces from "lower-priority" areas rather than from Donetsk Oblast, limiting the intended diversion [^1].
- Defence researcher Marina Miron described the offensive as a strategic failure for Ukraine [^1].
- Russian authorities reported hundreds of civilian deaths and mass displacement in the border area, although figures from both sides are unverified [^1].
- The territory did not survive as a bargaining chip, and Russian forces later opened a 2025 offensive into Ukraine's Sumy Oblast [^1].

## Recent News
On 26 April 2026, the first anniversary of Russia's declared recapture of Kursk, North Korea opened a memorial museum in Pyongyang for soldiers killed in "overseas military operations", attended by Kim Jong-un and Russian Defence Minister Andrey Belousov. South Korean intelligence estimated that up to 6,000 North Korean troops were killed or wounded in Kursk, and neither government has published official figures [^4].
