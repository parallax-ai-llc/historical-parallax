## Summary

George Burns (January 20, 1896 – March 9, 1996) was an American comedian, actor, singer and writer whose career spanned vaudeville, radio, television and film across more than seventy years. He is best remembered as half of the comedy duo Burns and Allen, alongside his wife Gracie Allen, and for a late-career revival that made him the oldest Academy Award winner of his time.

Born Nathan Birnbaum on New York's Lower East Side, Burns spent decades as a journeyman vaudevillian before finding lasting success as Allen's straight man. After her retirement and death, he rebuilt himself as a solo performer in his late seventies and eighties, winning an Oscar for *The Sunshine Boys* (1975) and headlining the *Oh, God!* films.

With his ever-present cigar, deadpan delivery and jokes about his own longevity, Burns became a symbol of graceful ageing in American popular culture. He died at 100, seven weeks after his centennial birthday.

## Early Life & Background

Burns was born Nathan Birnbaum in New York City on January 20, 1896, the ninth of twelve children of Jewish immigrants Eliezer and Hadassah Birnbaum. His father died in 1903, leaving the family in poverty. Nathan left school in the fourth grade and worked shining shoes, running errands and selling newspapers.

At around age seven he formed the Pee-Wee Quartet with other children, singing for coins in saloons and on street corners. He adopted the stage name George Burns as a teenager and spent years in vaudeville, working as a singer, dancer, trick roller-skater and comic in a string of small-time acts.

## Rise to Prominence

Burns met Gracie Allen in 1922 and the two formed a double act, with Burns as the comic and Allen as the straight woman. Audiences laughed harder at Allen's questions than at his answers, so Burns reversed the roles, creating the scatterbrained Gracie and the patient George that would define their careers. They married on January 7, 1926.

The pair moved from vaudeville to radio in 1932 and hosted their own program from 1934 until 1950. *The George Burns and Gracie Allen Show* then ran on CBS television from 1950 to 1958, producing 291 episodes. Burns frequently stepped out of scenes to address the audience directly, a fourth-wall device that predated later sitcom experiments.

## Key Achievements

- **Burns and Allen** were one of the few acts to succeed in vaudeville, radio and television in succession.
- After Allen retired in 1958 and died on August 27, 1964, Burns produced television and worked in nightclubs.
- In 1975 he replaced his late friend Jack Benny in *The Sunshine Boys* and won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actor at 80, then the oldest winner in that category.
- He starred as the title character in *Oh, God!* (1977) and its two sequels, and in *Going in Style* (1979) and *18 Again!* (1988).
- His 1980 country single "I Wish I Was Eighteen Again" reached the Billboard country chart, and he won a Grammy Award in 1991 for the spoken-word recording of his memoir *Gracie: A Love Story*.
- He received the Kennedy Center Honors in 1988.

## Controversies & Criticism

Burns's public image as a devoted husband was complicated by his own admission, in later interviews and memoirs, of a brief affair in the early 1950s. He said he never confessed it to Allen but bought her an expensive gift out of guilt.

Some critics felt his late-career persona leaned heavily on a single joke about his age, and his willingness to be marketed as a cigar-smoking centenarian drew comment from health advocates. Others noted that Allen's comic talent had carried the act while Burns received the writing and producing credit, a point he acknowledged himself, often saying that his job was simply to ask "Gracie, how's your brother?"

## Legacy & Influence

Burns's direct-address narration on his 1950s series influenced later comedies that broke the fourth wall. His late resurgence made him a template for older performers seeking second acts, and he was booked to play the London Palladium for his 100th birthday before a 1994 fall left him too frail to perform.

He died at his Beverly Hills home on March 9, 1996, at age 100, and was interred beside Allen at Forest Lawn Memorial Park in Glendale. Their crypt reads "Gracie Allen & George Burns—Together Again." His sign-off, "Say goodnight, Gracie," remains one of the most quoted lines in American broadcasting.
