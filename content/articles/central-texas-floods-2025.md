## Summary

Over the Fourth of July weekend in 2025, torrential overnight rain sent flash floods through the Texas Hill Country, with the worst destruction along the Guadalupe River in Kerr County [^1]. Officials counted at least 135 deaths statewide, 116 of them in Kerr County, at the time Governor Greg Abbott gave an update in mid-July [^2]. Among the dead were 27 campers and counselors at Camp Mystic, an all-girls Christian summer camp beside the river [^3]. Wikipedia describes it as the deadliest inland flooding in the United States since the 1976 Big Thompson River flood, and it set off a national argument about flood warnings, camp safety, and federal emergency staffing [^1].

## Background

The Hill Country is often called "Flash Flood Alley." Steep limestone terrain and thin soils send rainfall quickly into narrow river valleys [^1]. Kerr County had no outdoor flood sirens or dedicated warning system. County Judge Rob Kelly said residents had balked at the cost of earlier proposals, while neighboring Comfort in Kendall County had sirens and reported no deaths [^4]. Kerr County had applied for state grants for warning equipment in 2017 and 2018 and was turned down both times [^1]. At the National Weather Service (NWS), the San Antonio-Austin office had several vacancies, and its warning coordination meteorologist had taken early retirement in April 2025 amid federal staff cuts [^4].

## The Event

The NWS issued a flood watch for the area at 1:18 p.m. CDT on 3 July [^1]. Rain fell overnight at rates above 5 inches per hour in places, and one gauge near Mason recorded 20.33 inches in total [^1]. The NWS escalated its alerts through the early hours of 4 July. It issued a flash flood warning with a "considerable" damage tag at about 1:14 a.m. and flash flood emergencies for Hunt and Ingram at 4:03 a.m. [^5]. At Hunt the Guadalupe rose about 26 feet in 45 minutes and crested at a record 37.52 feet [^1].

The water struck riverside camps, RV parks, and homes while most people were asleep. Camp Mystic later confirmed that 27 campers and counselors had died [^3]. More than 400 people were rescued, many by helicopter, and search teams from over a dozen states worked along the river for weeks [^1]. The death toll passed 100 by 8 July [^8].

## Response and Aftermath

Abbott issued a state disaster declaration on 4 July, and President Donald Trump approved a federal major disaster declaration for Kerr County on 6 July [^1]. The Texas Legislature took up flood response in special session. Abbott then signed Senate Bill 1 ("Heaven's 27") and House Bill 1 (the "Camper Act"). The laws require camps to keep emergency alert systems and evacuation plans, keep cabins out of floodplains, and submit to state inspections [^6]. A companion measure funds outdoor warning sirens in flood-prone areas [^7]. Lieutenant Governor Dan Patrick set up select committees on disaster preparedness [^1].

## Positive Perspectives

- Volunteers, state agencies, and teams from many states, along with the Coast Guard, carried out hundreds of rescues in the first days [^1].
- Officials in counties next to Kerr County said the NWS warnings were sufficient, and the New Braunfels forecast office had extra forecasters on duty during the storm [^4].
- Comfort's siren system, which the community paid for, triggered evacuations, and no deaths were reported there. Supporters of sirens point to it as proof that local warning systems work [^4].
- Families of Camp Mystic victims pushed for new camp-safety laws, and Abbott signed them with the families present [^7].
- Charities and businesses raised tens of millions of dollars for recovery. The Community Foundation of the Texas Hill Country alone reported about $30 million within a week [^1].

## Negative Perspectives

- Kerr County had rejected or failed to fund flood sirens for years despite the valley's known history of floods [^4].
- Critics linked NWS vacancies to federal staff cuts. NWS officials and some local leaders countered that the warnings went out on time [^4].
- Wikipedia's account of the event reports that Camp Mystic's director received the 1:14 a.m. warning but evacuations did not begin until about 2:30 a.m. It also reports that FEMA flood maps had removed dozens of camp buildings from the designated hazard area after appeals [^1].
- Reports cited in the same account say FEMA's search and call-center support was slowed by a new requirement that the Homeland Security secretary personally approve large contracts. Secretary Kristi Noem dismissed those reports [^1].
- Kerr County's CodeRED emergency alerts went out late. Some messages reached residents hours after the river had crested [^1].
