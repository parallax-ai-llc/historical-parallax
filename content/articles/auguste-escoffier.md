## Summary

Georges Auguste Escoffier (28 October 1846 – 12 February 1935) was a French chef, restaurateur and author who is widely regarded as the founder of modern professional cooking. He simplified and codified the elaborate haute cuisine inherited from Marie-Antoine Carême, reorganised the professional kitchen into the brigade system still used today, and published *Le Guide Culinaire* (1903), a reference work of roughly 5,000 recipes that remains a standard text in culinary schools.

Working in partnership with hotelier César Ritz, Escoffier ran the kitchens of the Savoy (1890–1898) and Carlton (1899–1920) hotels in London, where he cooked for royalty, aristocrats and celebrities and helped establish the luxury hotel restaurant as an institution. He was the first chef to be named a Chevalier (1920) and later Officer (1928) of the Légion d'honneur, and was popularly styled "the king of chefs and the chef of kings."

## Early Life & Background

Escoffier was born in Villeneuve-Loubet, a village in the Alpes-Maritimes near Nice, the son of a blacksmith. He hoped to become a sculptor, but at age 13, in 1859, he was apprenticed at his uncle's Restaurant Français in Nice. He worked there and in other Nice establishments until 1865, when he moved to Paris to join Le Petit Moulin Rouge, then one of the city's most fashionable restaurants.

During the Franco-Prussian War (1870–1871) Escoffier served as an army chef at headquarters in Metz and was held as a prisoner of war after the city fell. The experience prompted his early studies of canning and preserved food. In 1878 he married Delphine Daffis, a poet, with whom he had three children.

## Rise to Prominence

In 1884 Escoffier began working with César Ritz at the Grand Hôtel in Monte Carlo during the winter season and the Hôtel National in Lucerne in summer. When Richard D'Oyly Carte hired Ritz to manage the new Savoy Hotel in London in 1890, Escoffier took charge of the kitchens. The Savoy became the centre of London society dining, and Escoffier's dishes for its patrons, including Pêche Melba for the soprano Nellie Melba (c. 1893), made his name internationally.

After leaving the Savoy in 1898, Escoffier and Ritz opened the Hôtel Ritz in Paris the same year and the Carlton Hotel in London in 1899. Escoffier directed the Carlton kitchens, serving up to 500 diners a night, until his retirement in 1920. He also designed the kitchens for the Hamburg-Amerika Line's ocean liners, and in 1913 cooked for Kaiser Wilhelm II aboard the *Imperator*.

## Key Achievements

- **Le Guide Culinaire (1903)**: co-written with Philéas Gilbert and Émile Fétu, it systematised French cooking and reduced the sauce repertoire to five "mother sauces." It was revised through 1921 and is still in print.
- **Brigade de cuisine**: he divided kitchen labour into specialised stations (saucier, garde-manger, pâtissier and others) under a chef de cuisine, cutting duplication and speeding service.
- **Service à la russe**: he championed serving courses in sequence rather than all at once, and printed à la carte menus.
- **Other works**: *Le Livre des Menus* (1912), *L'Aide-mémoire culinaire* (1919), *Le Riz* (1927), *La Morue* (1929) and *Ma Cuisine* (1934), aimed at home cooks.
- **Professional welfare**: he founded the magazine *Le Carnet d'Épicure* (1911), the Ligue des Gourmands, and supported mutual-aid funds and pensions for cooks.

## Controversies & Criticism

In March 1898 the Savoy's board dismissed Ritz, Escoffier and manager Louis Echenard. An internal investigation found that Escoffier had accepted gifts and commissions from suppliers; he signed a confession acknowledging over £8,000 in kickbacks and disappearing stock, and repaid a portion. The affair was kept private and only became public through archival research in the 1980s.

Critics have also noted that his standardisation of French cuisine, however influential, tended to fix a hierarchy of dishes and techniques that later movements such as nouvelle cuisine in the 1970s explicitly reacted against.

## Legacy & Influence

Escoffier died in Monte Carlo on 12 February 1935, days after his wife. His methods shaped hotel and restaurant kitchens worldwide, and his terminology remains the common language of professional cooking. The Fondation Auguste Escoffier opened a museum in his birthplace in 1966, and the Auguste Escoffier School of Culinary Arts, founded in the United States in 2011, carries his name. The Disciples d'Escoffier, an international association of chefs founded in 1954, continues to promote his principles.
