## Summary

Ida B. Wells (1862–1931) was an American investigative journalist, educator, and civil rights activist who led the first sustained national campaign against lynching in the United States. Born into slavery in Mississippi months before the Emancipation Proclamation, she built a career as a newspaper editor in Memphis and, after a mob murdered three of her friends in 1892, turned her pen toward documenting and exposing racial violence.

Her pamphlets *Southern Horrors* (1892) and *A Red Record* (1895) used statistics and case records to dismantle the claim that lynching punished Black men for assaulting white women. She carried the campaign to Britain, co-founded the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP), and organized for women's suffrage in Chicago. In 2020 she received a posthumous Pulitzer Prize Special Citation.

## Early Life & Background

Wells was born on July 16, 1862, in Holly Springs, Mississippi, to James and Elizabeth Wells, both enslaved until the end of the Civil War. Her father, a carpenter, became a trustee of Rust College, which she attended. In 1878 a yellow fever epidemic killed both parents and an infant brother. At 16, Wells took a teaching job in a rural school to keep her five surviving siblings together.

Around 1882 she moved to Memphis, Tennessee, to teach and to study at Fisk University during summers. In 1884 she sued the Chesapeake, Ohio and Southwestern Railroad after being forcibly removed from a first-class ladies' car. She won $500 in a local court, but the Tennessee Supreme Court reversed the decision in 1887.

## Rise to Prominence

Wells began writing about the railroad case for Black church newspapers under the pen name "Iola." By 1889 she was co-owner and editor of the *Memphis Free Speech and Headlight*. In 1891 the Memphis school board dismissed her after she published criticism of conditions in Black schools.

The turning point came on March 9, 1892, when a mob lynched Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and Will Stewart, owners of the People's Grocery in Memphis. Moss was a close friend. Wells's editorials urged Black residents to leave the city and questioned the "rape myth" used to justify lynching. In May 1892, while she was traveling, a white mob destroyed the *Free Speech* office. She never returned to Memphis, settling first in New York and later in Chicago.

## Key Achievements

- **Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases** (1892) and **A Red Record** (1895) compiled press reports on lynchings, showing that fewer than one third involved even an accusation of rape.
- Lecture tours of Britain in 1893 and 1894 generated international pressure and led to the founding of the British Anti-Lynching Committee.
- In 1895 she married attorney Ferdinand Barnett and became co-owner of the *Chicago Conservator*, the city's first Black newspaper.
- In 1909 she was among the founders of the NAACP, though she was initially omitted from the published list of founders.
- She founded the Alpha Suffrage Club in 1913, the first Black women's suffrage organization in Illinois, and marched in the 1913 Washington suffrage parade despite being told to walk at the rear.
- In 1930 she ran unsuccessfully for the Illinois State Senate.

## Controversies & Criticism

Wells's uncompromising style put her at odds with prominent allies. Booker T. Washington considered her too confrontational, and she in turn criticized his accommodationist approach. Her relationship with the NAACP leadership, including W. E. B. Du Bois, was strained, and she felt marginalized within the organization she helped create. Frances Willard of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union clashed with her publicly after Wells accused Willard of tolerating Southern lynching. Some white suffrage leaders viewed her insistence on integration as a threat to Southern support for the movement.

## Legacy & Influence

Wells died of kidney disease in Chicago on March 25, 1931, at age 68. Her autobiography, *Crusade for Justice*, edited by her daughter Alfreda Duster, appeared in 1970. Her methods of data-driven investigative reporting influenced later muckraking and civil rights journalism. Chicago renamed Congress Parkway as Ida B. Wells Drive in 2019, and the Ida B. Wells Monument was unveiled in Bronzeville in 2021. The 2020 Pulitzer Special Citation recognized her "outstanding and courageous reporting" on lynching.
