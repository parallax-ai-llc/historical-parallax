## Summary
At 1:28 a.m. on 26 March 2024, the Singapore-flagged container ship MV Dali lost power and struck a pier of the Francis Scott Key Bridge in Baltimore, Maryland, bringing down most of the 2.6 km span within seconds [^1]. Six members of a road maintenance crew working on the bridge were killed [^1][^2]. The collapse closed the shipping channel to the Port of Baltimore for weeks and removed a major link in the region's highway network [^1]. Investigators later traced the blackout to a single loose wire connection, and the disaster produced record legal settlements and a multibillion-dollar rebuild [^3][^4].

## Background
The Key Bridge, a steel truss bridge opened in 1977, carried Interstate 695 across the Patapsco River at the entrance to one of the busiest ports on the US East Coast [^1]. The Dali was owned by Grace Ocean Private Ltd and managed by Synergy Marine Group [^1]. It had just left port bound for Colombo, Sri Lanka, when it lost electrical power [^1].

## The Event
The Dali suffered a blackout shortly after departure, briefly regained power, and then lost it again, leaving it without propulsion or steering [^1][^3]. The ship issued a mayday call at about 1:27 a.m., the pilot asked that traffic be stopped, and Maryland Transportation Authority police halted vehicles in both directions in the moments before the collapse [^1]. Less than a minute later the ship hit a support pier and the central spans fell into the river [^1]. The workers killed were employees of Brawner Builders who had been filling potholes on the roadway [^2]. One worker, Julio Cervantes Suarez, was rescued from the water [^5].

## Response and Aftermath
Federal, state, and military salvage teams cut apart the wreckage, and the Fort McHenry federal channel was fully reopened after about 11 weeks [^1]. On 24 October 2024 the Justice Department announced that Grace Ocean and Synergy Marine would pay $101,980,000 to settle federal civil claims for response costs [^6]. In December 2024 Congress approved about $2 billion in federal funding for reconstruction [^1]. The NTSB's final report, adopted on 18 November 2025, found that a signal wire had been improperly seated in a terminal block because a label band prevented its connector from being fully inserted; when it came loose, the ship lost power [^3][^1]. The Maryland Transportation Authority maintains that the Dali's owner and operator are solely responsible [^2].

## Positive Perspectives
- Police responded to the mayday by halting traffic onto the bridge before it fell, limiting the number of people exposed [^1].
- The federal shipping channel was cleared and reopened in about 11 weeks, restoring access to the Port of Baltimore [^1].
- The federal government recovered nearly $102 million in response costs from the ship's owner and operator [^6].
- Maryland reached a $2.25 billion settlement with the owner and operator in May 2026, described in reporting as a record [^4][^7].
- The families of the six workers reached wrongful-death settlements before the civil trial began [^5].

## Negative Perspectives
- The NTSB found that thermal scanning, which could have detected loose connections, was not used in the Dali's routine maintenance [^2].
- The rebuild's estimated cost has risen from about $1.7 to $1.9 billion to between $4.3 and $5.2 billion, with completion pushed to late 2030 [^1][^2].
- The owners sought to cap their liability at about $44 million under an 1851 federal law, the claimed value of the vessel and its freight [^5].
- Federal prosecutors filed criminal charges against Synergy entities and the ship's technical superintendent in May 2026, including allegations of providing false information to investigators [^4][^8].
- Questions remain about pier protection on older bridges; Maryland says the bridge complied with its federal permits [^2].

## Recent News
On 12 May 2026 Maryland announced a $2.25 billion settlement with Grace Ocean and Synergy Marine, the same day federal prosecutors filed criminal charges against Synergy companies and technical superintendent Radhakrishnan Karthik Nair [^7]. More than 30 civil settlements had been reached by late May 2026 [^5]. On 1 June 2026 Judge James Bredar postponed the limitation-of-liability trial to consider remaining economic-loss claims [^8].
