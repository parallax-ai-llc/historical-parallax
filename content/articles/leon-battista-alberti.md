## Summary

Leon Battista Alberti (14 February 1404 – 25 April 1472) was an Italian architect, author, and humanist scholar of the early Renaissance. He wrote the first modern treatises on painting, sculpture, and architecture, and he designed buildings in Rimini, Florence, and Mantua that reintroduced the vocabulary of ancient Roman architecture to fifteenth-century Italy.

Alberti mattered because he turned artistic practice into theory. His *De pictura* (1435) gave painters a written method for linear perspective, and his *De re aedificatoria* (completed around 1452) became the first architectural treatise of the Renaissance, modeled on Vitruvius but written for contemporary patrons and builders. Later generations treated him as the archetype of the universal man, a scholar who could also draw, build, and write Latin comedy.

His buildings are few, and several were finished by others, but their facades and proportional systems shaped European architecture for centuries.

## Early Life & Background

Alberti was born in Genoa on 14 February 1404, an illegitimate son of Lorenzo Alberti, a member of a wealthy Florentine banking family that had been exiled from Florence in 1387. He grew up in Genoa and Venice, and studied at the humanist school of Gasparino Barzizza in Padua, where he learned classical Latin and Greek.

He then studied canon law at the University of Bologna, earning a doctorate around 1428. His father died in 1421, and relatives disputed the inheritance, leaving him with limited resources. During his student years he wrote *Philodoxeos*, a Latin comedy that he circulated as a rediscovered ancient text, an early sign of his facility with classical forms. The ban on the Alberti family was lifted in 1428, allowing him to visit Florence, where he encountered the work of Brunelleschi, Donatello, and Masaccio.

## Rise to Prominence

Alberti took minor holy orders and, by 1432, held a post as a secretary in the papal chancery in Rome. Service to the Curia gave him income, time to study Roman ruins, and access to patrons across Italy. He produced *Descriptio urbis Romae*, an early survey of the ancient city using a coordinate system.

In 1435 he wrote *De pictura* in Latin, followed by an Italian version dedicated to Brunelleschi in 1436. The treatise formalized one-point perspective and set out rules for composition, light, and the *istoria*, or narrative subject. His Italian dialogue *Della famiglia* (written 1433–1441) discussed household economy and education. He also wrote *De statua* on sculpture and the moral dialogue *Momus*.

## Key Achievements

- **De re aedificatoria** (c. 1452, printed 1485): ten books on architecture covering materials, construction, city planning, and proportion. It set out the principle of *concinnitas*, harmony of parts, and became the standard reference for Renaissance architects.
- **Tempio Malatestiano, Rimini** (begun c. 1450): a marble encasement of the church of San Francesco for Sigismondo Malatesta, with a facade modeled on a Roman triumphal arch. It was left unfinished.
- **Palazzo Rucellai, Florence** (c. 1446–1451): a facade with three superimposed orders of pilasters, the first Renaissance palace front to apply the classical orders systematically.
- **Santa Maria Novella, Florence** (facade completed 1470): Alberti resolved the church front with a proportional grid of squares and introduced the large scrolls linking nave and aisle roofs, a device copied for two centuries.
- **San Sebastiano** (begun 1460) and **Sant'Andrea, Mantua** (designed 1470, begun 1472): Sant'Andrea combined a temple front with a triumphal arch and a barrel-vaulted nave that influenced later church design, including Il Gesù in Rome.

## Controversies & Criticism

Alberti's authorship and execution have been debated. He rarely supervised construction, relying on builders such as Bernardo Rossellino and Luca Fancelli, so scholars dispute how much of Palazzo Rucellai or Sant'Andrea reflects his intentions. Critics from the sixteenth century onward, including Vasari, praised his writing while noting that his built output was small compared with his reputation.

His *Della famiglia* contains views on women and household authority that modern readers find restrictive, and his pretended "rediscovery" of *Philodoxeos* has been described as a literary hoax. His autobiography, written in the third person, is also regarded as self-promoting.

## Legacy & Influence

Alberti's treatises gave the Renaissance its theoretical foundation. *De pictura* shaped how painters from Piero della Francesca to Leonardo da Vinci understood perspective, and *De re aedificatoria* was translated into Italian, French, Spanish, and English, guiding architects such as Bramante, Palladio, and later Christopher Wren. Jacob Burckhardt, in 1860, presented him as the model of the many-sided Renaissance individual. His facades in Florence, Rimini, and Mantua remain reference points in the history of classical architecture.
