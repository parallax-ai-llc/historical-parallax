## Summary

In the early hours of 3 January 2026, the United States bombed military targets around Caracas. In a raid code-named Operation Absolute Resolve, Delta Force soldiers seized Venezuelan president Nicolas Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, and flew them to New York [^1][^2]. The couple were arraigned in Manhattan on narco-terrorism and drug trafficking charges and pleaded not guilty [^1][^3]. Venezuelan officials put the death toll at around 80 to 100, including 32 Cuban military and intelligence personnel. Seven US soldiers were wounded [^1]. UN officials and many legal experts called the raid a violation of the UN Charter. The operation reshaped Venezuela's government and opened its oil sector to the United States [^1][^4].

## Background

Maduro succeeded Hugo Chavez in 2013 and was widely accused of authoritarian rule. His declared victory in the 2024 election was condemned as fraudulent by the opposition and many international observers [^1]. US prosecutors had pursued narco-terrorism charges against him since at least 2020 [^1]. From August 2025 the United States built up forces in the southern Caribbean and struck vessels it accused of drug trafficking. In December 2025 it began seizing sanctioned oil tankers [^1]. According to Wikipedia's account of press reports, Maduro rejected an offer to go into exile in Turkey in late December [^1].

## The Event

According to Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine, President Donald Trump gave the order late on 2 January [^1]. At about 2 am local time on 3 January, explosions hit targets in Caracas, Miranda, Aragua, and La Guaira, including La Carlota air base and Fuerte Tiuna. The strikes lasted about 30 minutes [^2]. Wikipedia reports that about 150 aircraft took part, and that FBI agents accompanied Delta Force to formally arrest Maduro at his compound [^1]. Trump announced that Maduro and his wife had been "captured and flown out of the Country" [^2]. The couple were taken to the USS Iwo Jima and landed at Stewart Air National Guard Base in New York that evening [^1].

Casualty figures varied. Cuba said 32 of its personnel were killed. On 16 January Venezuela's defence minister said 47 Venezuelan soldiers had died, and independent reports also confirmed civilian deaths [^1]. The Pentagon said seven US soldiers were wounded [^1].

## Response and Aftermath

Attorney General Pam Bondi announced an indictment in the Southern District of New York on charges including narco-terrorism conspiracy and cocaine importation conspiracy [^1]. At his arraignment on 5 January, Maduro said he considered himself "a prisoner of war" [^1]. Vice President Delcy Rodriguez denounced the "kidnapping" but was sworn in as acting president on 5 January, and the government remained in place [^1]. UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said he was "deeply concerned" about instability and questioned whether international law had been respected. At the Security Council, US Ambassador Mike Waltz cited the right to self-defence under Article 51 [^4]. Washington and Caracas restored diplomatic relations, and the US embassy reopened [^1].

## Positive Perspectives

- Supporters said the operation removed a leader whom critics at home and abroad had long accused of jailing opponents and rigging elections. Members of the Venezuelan diaspora celebrated [^1].
- Hundreds of political prisoners were released after January. An amnesty law passed on 19 February, and rights groups counted 621 confirmed releases by 8 March [^1].
- The United States lifted many sanctions on Venezuelan oil and restored diplomatic ties, which allowed oil trade to resume [^1].
- Maduro is being tried in a US federal court with defence counsel, and a trial date has been set [^3].

## Negative Perspectives

- Legal experts said the raid lacked Security Council authorization, Venezuelan consent, or a self-defence justification. Colombia's UN ambassador called it an act of aggression [^4].
- University of Pennsylvania professor Bill Burke-White called the operation "illegal under international law in every imaginable way." He also argued that Maduro enjoyed sovereign immunity as Venezuela's leader [^5].
- Critics said oil was a central motive. Trump said openly that access to Venezuelan oil mattered, and Wikipedia cites The New York Times as describing Venezuela as a de facto US-controlled state since the raid [^1].
- Elections have not been held. On 24 July 2026 Trump said Venezuela was not ready for a vote [^1].

## Recent News

At a hearing on 22 July 2026, US District Judge Alvin Hellerstein set Maduro and Flores's trial for 1 June 2027. He set a 2 September deadline for initial motions to dismiss and scheduled arguments for 17 November [^3]. Defence lawyer Barry Pollack said he would seek dismissal on head-of-state immunity grounds and challenge the legality of the capture [^3]. Delcy Rodriguez remains acting president [^1].
