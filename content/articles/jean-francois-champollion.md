## Summary

Jean-François Champollion (23 December 1790 – 4 March 1832) was a French philologist and orientalist who is widely credited as the founder of scientific Egyptology. His decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs, announced in Paris in September 1822, resolved a puzzle that had defeated European scholars for centuries and opened more than three millennia of ancient Egyptian written history to modern study.

Working from the Rosetta Stone, the Philae obelisk, and copies of temple inscriptions, Champollion demonstrated that hieroglyphic writing combined phonetic signs with ideographic and determinative signs. This insight overturned the long-held assumption that the script was purely symbolic. His subsequent grammar and dictionary of the language established the framework that later Egyptologists refined but never replaced.

Champollion died at 41, before most of his work was published. Even so, his name remains synonymous with the birth of Egyptology, and the "Champollion" designation is attached to museums, streets, and institutions in France and Egypt.

## Early Life & Background

Champollion was born in Figeac, in the Lot department of southwestern France, the youngest of seven children of a bookseller. His older brother Jacques-Joseph Champollion-Figeac, a scholar twelve years his senior, supervised his education and remained his lifelong supporter and editor.

From 1801 he lived with his brother in Grenoble, where he studied at the lycée and developed a precocious talent for languages. By his mid-teens he had studied Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, Chaldean, and Coptic. In 1807, at 16, he presented a paper to the Academy of Grenoble arguing that Coptic was the surviving form of the ancient Egyptian language. That hypothesis later became central to his decipherment.

He continued his studies in Paris from 1807 to 1809 under the orientalist Silvestre de Sacy at the Collège de France and the École spéciale des langues orientales. In 1809, aged 18, he was appointed assistant professor of history at Grenoble.

## Rise to Prominence

Champollion's political sympathies were with Napoleon, and after the Bourbon Restoration he lost his Grenoble post and was briefly exiled to Figeac in 1816. He returned to Grenoble in 1817 but was dismissed again in 1821 and moved to Paris.

During these years he competed, largely at a distance, with the English polymath Thomas Young, who had identified some phonetic values in the demotic and hieroglyphic texts of the Rosetta Stone. Champollion's decisive step came in 1822 when he compared the cartouches of Ptolemy and Cleopatra from the Rosetta Stone and the Bankes obelisk from Philae, and then read the non-Greek names Ramesses and Thutmose from Abu Simbel copies. This proved that phonetic signs were used for native Egyptian names, not only for foreign ones.

On 27 September 1822 he presented his findings to the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres in the "Lettre à M. Dacier". The fuller "Précis du système hiéroglyphique" followed in 1824.

## Key Achievements

- **Decipherment of hieroglyphs (1822–1824):** established that the script mixed phonetic, ideographic, and determinative signs, and produced a working alphabet.
- **Turin and Italian collections (1824–1826):** catalogued Egyptian antiquities in Turin, where he examined the Turin King List, and helped assemble the Louvre's Egyptian department, becoming its first curator in 1826.
- **Franco-Tuscan expedition (1828–1829):** led, with Ippolito Rosellini, the first scientific expedition to Egypt and Nubia, copying inscriptions and confirming his method in the field.
- **Chair of Egyptology (1831):** the Collège de France created the first chair in Egyptian antiquities for him.
- **Posthumous works:** his "Grammaire égyptienne" (1836–1841) and "Dictionnaire égyptien" (1841–1843) were edited by his brother.

## Controversies & Criticism

The most persistent dispute concerns priority. Thomas Young published partial phonetic readings in 1819 and argued that Champollion had built on his results without adequate credit. Champollion acknowledged Young's work only grudgingly, and the disagreement took on nationalist overtones between British and French scholars. Most historians now assign Young credit for early insights and Champollion for the systematic breakthrough.

During the 1828–1829 expedition, Champollion authorised the removal of two painted reliefs from the tomb of Seti I in the Valley of the Kings, now in the Louvre and in Florence. Critics have described this as vandalism of a well-preserved monument. His readings were also contested during his lifetime by scholars such as Julius Klaproth, and some of his early sign values were later corrected.

## Legacy & Influence

Champollion's method made possible the reading of Egyptian texts from the Old Kingdom to the Roman period, transforming Egypt from a subject of speculation into a documented civilisation. Karl Richard Lepsius, who systematised the grammar in the 1830s, built directly on his work.

The Musée Champollion in Figeac, his birthplace, is devoted to the history of writing, and a second museum in Vif near Grenoble preserves his family home. His name is carried by lycées in France and Egypt, by a Paris street beside the Collège de France, and by a lunar crater.
