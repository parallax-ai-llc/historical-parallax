## Summary

Lech Wałęsa is a Polish electrician, trade union organizer, and statesman who led the Solidarity movement, the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc. Born on 29 September 1943, he rose from the Gdańsk shipyards to become the face of peaceful resistance to communist rule in Poland during the 1980s.

He was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983 and served as President of Poland from 1990 to 1995, the first popularly elected head of state in the country's post-war history. His leadership of the 1980 strikes and the 1989 Round Table negotiations helped set in motion the collapse of communist governments across Central and Eastern Europe.

Wałęsa remains a divisive figure in Poland. Admirers credit him with a decisive role in ending one-party rule without bloodshed, while critics point to his confrontational presidency and long-running allegations that he informed for the secret police in the early 1970s.

## Early Life & Background

Wałęsa was born in Popowo, a village in German-occupied Poland, to a family of farmers. His father, Bolesław, died in 1945 after being held in a Nazi labor camp. Wałęsa attended vocational school in Chalin and Lipno, trained as an electrician, and completed military service between 1963 and 1965. In 1967 he took a job at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk, where he worked on ship electrical systems. He married Danuta Gołoś in 1969, and the couple had eight children.

## Rise to Prominence

Wałęsa took part in the December 1970 shipyard protests, in which security forces killed more than 40 workers along the Baltic coast. He was dismissed from the shipyard in 1976 after criticizing management and spent the late 1970s working in smaller firms while organizing with the illegal Free Trade Unions of the Coast, which brought repeated arrests.

On 14 August 1980, when a strike began at the Lenin Shipyard over the firing of crane operator Anna Walentynowicz, Wałęsa climbed the shipyard fence to join and soon lead it. As chairman of the Inter-Enterprise Strike Committee, he signed the Gdańsk Agreement with the government on 31 August 1980, securing the legal right to form independent unions. Solidarity grew to roughly 10 million members within a year.

## Key Achievements

- Led Solidarity through its legal period and after martial law was declared on 13 December 1981, when he was interned for 11 months.
- Received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983. His wife accepted it on his behalf because he feared being denied re-entry to Poland.
- Represented the opposition at the Round Table talks of February to April 1989, which produced partially free elections on 4 June 1989. Solidarity won 99 of 100 Senate seats.
- Elected President of Poland on 9 December 1990 with about 74 percent of the second-round vote. He oversaw the withdrawal of Soviet troops, completed in 1993, and early steps toward NATO membership.
- Addressed a joint session of the United States Congress in November 1989, one of the few foreign non-heads of state to do so.

## Controversies & Criticism

Wałęsa's presidency was marked by frequent clashes with parliament and rapid turnover of prime ministers, and his blunt style alienated many former allies. He lost his 1995 re-election bid to Aleksander Kwaśniewski, and in 2000 received about 1 percent of the vote.

The most persistent controversy concerns "Bolek," a codename in secret police files. Documents released in 2016 from the home of former interior minister Czesław Kiszczak indicated that Wałęsa signed a cooperation agreement in 1970 and received payments. Wałęsa has denied being a knowing informant, and a 2000 lustration court cleared him, though a 2017 forensic report concluded the signatures were genuine. He has also drawn criticism for remarks about LGBT people in 2013 and for his feud with the Kaczyński brothers.

## Legacy & Influence

Wałęsa is widely regarded as a central figure of the peaceful revolutions of 1989, alongside Václav Havel and Mikhail Gorbachev. Gdańsk airport bears his name, and the European Solidarity Centre in Gdańsk documents the movement he led. He has received the Presidential Medal of Freedom, and Andrzej Wajda's 2013 film "Wałęsa: Man of Hope" dramatized his life. Since leaving office he has founded the Lech Wałęsa Institute and continued to comment on Polish and international politics.
