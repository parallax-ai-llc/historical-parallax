## Summary

Kerby Jean-Raymond is an American fashion designer and the founder of Pyer Moss, the New York label he launched in 2013. He is best known for turning runway shows into staged arguments about Black American history, police violence, mental health and labor, and for treating fashion as a form of activism rather than a decorative trade.

His importance rests on a small number of highly visible moments. In 2015 he opened a show with a film about police killings, in 2018 he won the CFDA/Vogue Fashion Fund, and in 2021 he became the first Black American designer invited to present on the official Paris haute couture calendar. Each of these events reshaped how the industry talked about who gets to make luxury and what luxury can be about.

Jean-Raymond has also worked as a corporate creative leader, most prominently at Reebok, and has drawn criticism for his management style and for the uneven quality of some collections. He remains one of the defining figures of the post-2015 conversation about race and representation in American fashion.

## Early Life & Background

Jean-Raymond was born on 22 November 1986 in East Flatbush, Brooklyn, to Haitian immigrant parents. His mother, Vania Moss-Pierre, died of lupus when he was seven, and he was raised by his father. The Pyer Moss name combines a variant of his mother's surname with his own middle name.

He attended the High School of Fashion Industries in Manhattan, where at 14 he began an internship with designer Kay Unger that turned into a paid job. He later studied business at Hofstra University and worked in menswear at Marc Jacobs, Kenneth Cole and Theory before founding his own label.

## Rise to Prominence

Pyer Moss debuted in 2013 as a menswear line. The label's breakthrough came at New York Fashion Week in September 2015, when Jean-Raymond opened his Spring 2016 show with a roughly ten-minute film compiling footage of police shootings of Black Americans. The show cost him a major investor but made him a national name. His February 2016 show addressed depression and financial stress, and he later said the label was close to insolvency at that point.

A partnership with Reebok, announced in 2017, stabilized the business. Between 2018 and 2019 he presented the three-part series "American, Also," staged at the Weeksville Heritage Center and the Kings Theatre in Brooklyn, with gospel choirs and paintings by Black artists on the garments. In November 2018 Pyer Moss won the CFDA/Vogue Fashion Fund and its $400,000 prize.

## Key Achievements

- Won the CFDA/Vogue Fashion Fund in November 2018.
- Named American Menswear Designer of the Year at the CFDA Fashion Awards in September 2020.
- Appointed Reebok's global creative director in September 2020, overseeing the brand's product and creative direction.
- Presented "Wat U Iz" on 10 July 2021 at Villa Lewaro, Madam C.J. Walker's estate in Irvington, New York, as the first Black American designer on the official Paris couture schedule. The collection dramatized 25 inventions by Black Americans, from the hair roller to the ice cream scoop.
- During the 2020 pandemic he converted the Pyer Moss studio into a donation hub and committed $50,000 in grants to minority- and women-owned small businesses.

## Controversies & Criticism

The 2015 police-brutality film divided the audience, and several trade writers argued that Jean-Raymond used politics as spectacle. The 2021 couture show, delayed two days by Tropical Storm Elsa, drew mixed reviews, with some critics calling the object-shaped garments closer to costume than couture.

Between 2022 and 2023 fashion press reported departures of senior staff, delayed payments to collaborators and complaints from former employees about a demanding workplace. Jean-Raymond disputed parts of the reporting. Pyer Moss also went quiet for extended periods, and his Reebok tenure ended after the brand's 2022 sale by Adidas to Authentic Brands Group, prompting questions about the label's stability.

## Legacy & Influence

Jean-Raymond helped move Black history from the margins of fashion storytelling to its center at a moment when the industry was under pressure to diversify. His shows established a template, later followed by other designers, in which a collection is presented as a thesis about culture rather than a product drop. He also demonstrated that a Brooklyn-based independent label could reach the couture calendar without a European conglomerate behind it, a precedent that continues to shape how young designers of color approach the industry.
