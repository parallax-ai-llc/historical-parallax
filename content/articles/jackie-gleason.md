## Summary

Jackie Gleason (1916–1987) was an American comedian, actor, and bandleader who became one of the defining stars of early television. Known as "The Great One," he created and played Ralph Kramden, the blustering Brooklyn bus driver of *The Honeymooners*, a sitcom whose 39 filmed episodes from 1955–56 remain among the most rerun programs in American broadcasting.

Beyond comedy, Gleason earned an Academy Award nomination for playing pool shark Minnesota Fats in *The Hustler* (1961), won a Tony Award for the musical *Take Me Along* (1960), and sold millions of mood-music albums under his own name. His catchphrases, including "And away we go!" and "How sweet it is!", entered everyday American speech.

His career spanned vaudeville, nightclubs, Broadway, film, records, and four decades of television, making him a rare performer who succeeded across nearly every entertainment medium of his era.

## Early Life & Background

Herbert Walton Gleason Jr. was born on February 26, 1916, in the Bushwick section of Brooklyn, New York. His older brother Clemence died at age 14 in 1919. His father, an insurance auditor, abandoned the family in 1925. His mother, Mae Kelly Gleason, supported them by working in a subway token booth until her death in 1935, when Gleason was 19.

Gleason attended P.S. 73 and Bushwick High School but dropped out. He won an amateur-night contest at the Halsey Theatre as a teenager and drifted into work as a master of ceremonies, carnival barker, and nightclub comic, eventually performing at Manhattan's Club 18.

## Rise to Prominence

Warner Bros. signed Gleason in 1940 and cast him in supporting roles in films such as *Navy Blues* (1941) and *Larceny, Inc.* (1942). Broadway followed, including the long-running musical *Follow the Girls* (1944).

Television made him a star. He played Chester A. Riley in NBC's *The Life of Riley* (1949–50), then took over DuMont's *Cavalcade of Stars* in 1950, where he introduced characters like Reginald Van Gleason III, Joe the Bartender, and Ralph Kramden. CBS hired him in 1952 for *The Jackie Gleason Show*, which ran until 1957 and again from 1962 to 1970, the later years broadcast from Miami Beach.

## Key Achievements

- *The Honeymooners* (1955–56), co-starring Art Carney, Audrey Meadows, and Joyce Randolph, was produced under a Buick-sponsored deal widely reported as the richest in television at the time.
- His debut album *Music for Lovers Only* (1952) spent 153 weeks in the Billboard top ten, launching a series of best-selling orchestral records despite Gleason being unable to read music.
- He won the 1960 Tony Award for Best Actor in a Musical for *Take Me Along*.
- His dramatic turn in *The Hustler* (1961) brought an Oscar nomination for Best Supporting Actor, followed by *Requiem for a Heavyweight* (1962) and *Gigot* (1962), which he also wrote.
- As Sheriff Buford T. Justice in *Smokey and the Bandit* (1977) and its two sequels, he reached a new generation of audiences. His final film was *Nothing in Common* (1986).

## Controversies & Criticism

Gleason was known for heavy drinking, a volatile temper, and a refusal to rehearse, which strained relationships with writers and co-stars. In 1952 CBS replaced original Alice Kramden actress Pert Kelton after she was blacklisted, a decision later criticized as capitulation to McCarthy-era pressure. His 1961 game show *You're in the Picture* was cancelled after one episode; Gleason spent the next week's broadcast apologizing on air. Ralph Kramden's threats to send Alice "to the moon" have drawn later criticism as domestic-violence humor, though defenders note Alice always won the argument. His first marriage to Genevieve Halford, separated for years before a 1970 divorce, also drew tabloid attention.

## Legacy & Influence

Gleason died of cancer on June 24, 1987, in Lauderhill, Florida, at age 71. The 1985 release of "lost" *Honeymooners* sketches from 1952–57 renewed interest in his work. Brooklyn's Jackie Gleason Bus Depot and a bronze statue of Ralph Kramden at the Port Authority Bus Terminal (2000) honor him in New York, while Miami Beach's Fillmore theater long bore his name. Ralph Kramden directly inspired Fred Flintstone, and Gleason's blend of working-class pathos and physical comedy shaped sitcom fathers from Archie Bunker to Homer Simpson.
