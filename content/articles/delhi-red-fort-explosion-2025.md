## Summary

On the evening of 10 November 2025, a white Hyundai i20 exploded in slow-moving traffic near Gate 1 of the Lal Qila (Red Fort) metro station in Old Delhi. The blast killed passers-by and set nearby vehicles on fire [^1][^2]. The death toll was later reported as 15, including the driver, but India's National Investigation Agency (NIA) has since put it at 11 victims [^4][^5]. Investigators linked the car to Umar Un Nabi, a doctor from Pulwama, and to a network of doctors in Faridabad and Kashmir. The day before, police had seized about 2,900 kg of explosive material from that network [^1][^3]. It was the most serious bombing in the Indian capital in years, and it led to one of the largest terrorism prosecutions in India in 2026 [^2][^5].

## Background

On 9 and 10 November 2025, police from Jammu and Kashmir and Haryana raided two rented houses in Faridabad, a city next to Delhi. They recovered roughly 350 kg and 2,563 kg of explosive material, along with rifles, timers, and bomb-making components [^1][^3]. Doctors from Kashmir were arrested, and police alleged links to cells of Jaish-e-Mohammed and Ansar Ghazwat-ul-Hind [^1]. Investigators later said the car's driver worked at the same Al-Falah University hospital in Faridabad as one of the arrested doctors [^1].

## The Event

According to Indian Express, CCTV footage showed the i20 parked near the Sunehri Masjid for more than three hours before it left the lot shortly before the blast [^7]. At about 6:52 pm IST the car stopped at a red light, moved off, and exploded. Delhi Police Commissioner Satish Golcha said "a slow-moving vehicle stopped at a red light" and that the explosion damaged nearby vehicles [^2]. The Delhi Fire Service said six cars, two e-rickshaws, and an auto-rickshaw burned, and the fire was under control by 7:29 pm [^1]. Al Jazeera's early report gave at least nine dead and about 20 wounded. Several injured victims died in hospital over the following week [^2][^4].

## Response and Aftermath

Police invoked the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act and the Explosives Act on the night of the blast, and security was raised across Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Mumbai, and major airports [^2]. Home Minister Amit Shah said investigators were "exploring all possibilities" [^2]. The Ministry of Home Affairs transferred the case to the NIA, and the federal Cabinet formally called the incident a terrorist act on 12 November [^1]. DNA analysis identified the driver as Umar Un Nabi, a doctor from Pulwama [^1][^5]. Investigators said he may have acted in panic after the Faridabad raids [^1]. His family home in Pulwama was demolished on 14 November. The Delhi government announced compensation of 1 million rupees for each victim's family [^1]. A later UN Security Council sanctions monitoring report attributed the attack to Al-Qaeda in the Indian Subcontinent (AQIS) [^1].

## Positive Perspectives

- Security agencies quickly linked the blast to the Faridabad explosives seizure. That seizure may itself have stopped a much larger planned attack [^1][^3].
- The NIA's 7,500-page chargesheet relies on 588 witness statements, DNA profiling, and voice analysis, and it lays out the alleged conspiracy in detail [^5].
- India's Press Information Bureau publicly debunked fake images and "false flag" claims circulating after the blast [^1].
- Authorities moved quickly to compensate victims' families and to raise security at crowded public places across the country [^1][^2].

## Negative Perspectives

- Casualty figures remain inconsistent. Media and Wikipedia report 15 dead, including the bomber, while NIA chargesheets refer to 11 people killed [^1][^4][^5].
- A car carrying a large bomb sat for hours in a parking lot near one of India's most heavily visited monuments without being detected [^7].
- The case raised questions about how a group of medical professionals at a private university could allegedly assemble tonnes of explosive material near the capital [^1][^5].
- Demolishing the suspect's family home in Kashmir before any trial drew attention to collective punishment practices [^1].

## Recent News

On 14 May 2026 the NIA filed a 7,500-page chargesheet before a special court at Patiala House, New Delhi, against 10 accused, five of them doctors. It alleges that the attack was carried out by an "AGuH Interim" faction inspired by AQIS, under a campaign called "Operation Heavenly Hind" [^5]. Charges include offences under the anti-terror law, the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, and the Explosive Substances Act. Proceedings against Umar Un Nabi are to abate because he died in the blast [^5]. In late June 2026 a supplementary chargesheet named three more men from Jammu and Kashmir, one of them still at large, bringing the number charged to 13 [^6].
