## Summary

On the evening of 26 September 2025, a lithium-ion battery fire broke out at the National Information Resources Service (NIRS) data center in Daejeon, the main hub for South Korea's government computer systems [^1]. Authorities took 647 government online services offline, including mobile ID, the Government24 portal, postal services, and parts of the 119 emergency text and video system [^1][^2]. The fire also destroyed the government's internal cloud storage service, G-Drive, which had no backup, and about 858 TB of civil servants' files were lost [^3]. The incident exposed how concentrated and fragile one of the world's most digitized governments was, and it led to criminal investigations and a political reckoning [^4][^5].

## Background

NIRS runs data centers in Daejeon, Gwangju, and Daegu that host much of the central government's IT infrastructure [^1]. The uninterruptible power supply (UPS) batteries involved had been installed in August 2014. That was past their recommended ten-year service life, and they were being moved from a server floor to the basement [^1]. G-Drive was the required document store for central government employees. Roughly 125,000 civil servants used it actively, each with about 30 GB of storage [^3].

## The Event

The fire started at about 8:20 p.m. on Friday, 26 September, in a fifth-floor server room. It began in a UPS battery that had been disconnected for the move [^1]. It damaged cooling and humidity-control systems, so officials shut down other systems as a precaution to stop servers from overheating [^2]. Firefighters fought the battery fire into the next day, and it had been fully put out by the time fire, police, and National Forensic Service investigators began their on-site inspection on Sunday morning, 28 September [^6]. Ninety-six systems were directly destroyed, and many more in undamaged rooms went offline because they depended on the damaged equipment [^1][^4].

## Response and Aftermath

Prime Minister Kim Min-seok apologized for the disruption on 27 September, and President Lee Jae Myung apologized at a Cabinet meeting [^2][^1]. The Ministry of the Interior and Safety put critical network repairs first and moved destroyed systems to the NIRS branch in Daegu [^1][^7]. Recovery was slow. By 2 October only 115 of 647 systems had been restored [^4]. By the end of October, about 90 percent of 709 affected services were working again [^7]. Police raided NIRS on 2 October and booked four people, one NIRS official and three outsourced workers, for professional negligence [^4]. Officials said some G-Drive documents might be recoverable through the separate Onnara records system [^3].

## Positive Perspectives

- Officials prioritized services essential to daily life, such as identity verification and emergency communications, and restored them first [^1].
- Private IT firms including Samsung SDS and LG CNS joined state research institutes to speed up repairs [^4].
- Top officials apologized publicly and quickly, including the prime minister and the president [^2][^1].
- Police opened a criminal investigation within a week and later referred numerous individuals and companies to prosecutors [^4][^5].

## Negative Perspectives

- About 858 TB of G-Drive data was permanently lost because the system had no backup. One official described eight years of work materials as "completely lost" [^3].
- The batteries were past their recommended service life, and experts suggested that workers may have removed wiring before cutting the power [^1].
- Restoration took more than a month. Even at the end of October, about one in ten affected services was still not back to normal [^7].
- The incident showed the risk of concentrating government systems in one facility without full disaster-recovery redundancy [^4].
- Police later cited management failures and construction safety lapses, and referred four companies under the electrical construction law [^5].

## Recent News

In March 2026, police declined to pursue a civic group's complaint against senior officials, including former NIRS director Lee Jae-yong. Prosecutors at the Daejeon District Prosecutors' Office later asked for a supplementary investigation into possible obstruction-of-business charges [^5][^8]. Separately, police referred Lee and 18 others, along with four companies, on charges including negligent fire and violations of the electrical construction law, and prosecutors were reviewing indictments as of June 2026 [^5].
