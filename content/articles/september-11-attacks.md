## Summary

On the morning of September 11, 2001, nineteen members of the Islamist militant group al-Qaeda hijacked four commercial passenger jets in the northeastern United States. Two aircraft, American Airlines Flight 11 and United Airlines Flight 175, were flown into the North and South Towers of the World Trade Center in New York City. A third, American Airlines Flight 77, struck the Pentagon in Arlington, Virginia. The fourth, United Airlines Flight 93, crashed into a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania, after passengers attempted to retake the cockpit.

The attacks killed 2,977 victims, plus the 19 hijackers, and injured more than 6,000 people. Both 110-story towers collapsed within two hours, destroying or damaging much of the surrounding complex. It remains the deadliest terrorist attack in history and the deadliest single incident for firefighters and police officers in the United States.

The attacks reshaped American foreign and domestic policy, prompting the invasion of Afghanistan, the creation of the Department of Homeland Security, and the passage of the USA PATRIOT Act. Their political, legal, and cultural consequences continue to shape international relations and civil liberties debates two decades later.

## Background & Causes

Al-Qaeda, founded by Osama bin Laden in 1988, emerged from the network of Arab volunteers who fought Soviet forces in Afghanistan. Bin Laden opposed the presence of U.S. troops in Saudi Arabia after the 1991 Gulf War, U.S. support for Israel, and sanctions on Iraq. In 1996 and 1998 he issued declarations calling for attacks on Americans. Al-Qaeda bombed the U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania in 1998, killing 224 people, and attacked the USS Cole in Yemen in 2000.

Khalid Sheikh Mohammed proposed the "planes operation" to bin Laden in 1996. Al-Qaeda approved the plan in 1999 and selected operatives, several of whom, including Mohamed Atta, trained as pilots in the United States during 2000 and 2001. U.S. intelligence agencies received warnings of a planned attack, including an August 6, 2001 presidential briefing titled "Bin Ladin Determined to Strike in US," but did not identify the plot.

## Key Figures

- **Osama bin Laden** – founder and leader of al-Qaeda, who authorized and financed the attacks.
- **Khalid Sheikh Mohammed** – principal architect of the plot, captured in Pakistan in 2003 and held at Guantánamo Bay.
- **Mohamed Atta** – tactical leader of the hijackers and pilot of Flight 11.
- **George W. Bush** – U.S. president, who launched the "war on terror" in response.
- **Rudolph Giuliani** – mayor of New York City during the attacks and recovery.
- **Todd Beamer and the passengers of Flight 93** – led the revolt that prevented the aircraft from reaching its likely target in Washington, D.C.
- **First responders** – 343 New York City firefighters, 37 Port Authority officers, and 23 NYPD officers died in the rescue effort.

## Consequences & Impact

Immediate responses included the grounding of all civilian air traffic in the United States for the first time in history and the closure of the New York Stock Exchange until September 17. Cleanup at Ground Zero continued until May 2002. Congress passed the USA PATRIOT Act in October 2001, expanding surveillance powers, and the Department of Homeland Security was created in 2002. The Transportation Security Administration federalized airport screening.

The U.S. invaded Afghanistan in October 2001 to remove the Taliban government sheltering al-Qaeda, beginning a war that lasted until 2021. The attacks were also cited in the case for the 2003 invasion of Iraq. Global counterterrorism cooperation expanded, and the Guantánamo Bay detention camp opened in 2002. Health effects on responders and residents exposed to toxic dust have caused thousands of additional illnesses and deaths, addressed by the James Zadroga 9/11 Health and Compensation Act of 2010.

## Perspectives

The 9/11 Commission concluded the attacks resulted from a "failure of imagination" and poor coordination between the CIA and FBI. Critics of the U.S. response argue the PATRIOT Act, enhanced interrogation, and the Iraq War undermined civil liberties and international law, while supporters credit expanded security measures with preventing further large-scale attacks on U.S. soil. Scholars of the Middle East debate the degree to which U.S. foreign policy contributed to al-Qaeda's rise. Conspiracy theories questioning the official account persist but have been rejected by engineering investigations, including the National Institute of Standards and Technology reports on the towers' collapse.
