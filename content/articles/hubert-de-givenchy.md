## Summary

Hubert James Marcel Taffin de Givenchy (20 February 1927 – 10 March 2018) was a French couturier who founded the House of Givenchy in Paris in 1952 and led it until his retirement in 1995. Born into an aristocratic family in Beauvais, he opened his own house at 24 and quickly became known for clean lines, restrained ornament, and an ideal of elegance he shared with his mentor, Cristóbal Balenciaga.

His name is inseparable from Audrey Hepburn, whom he dressed on screen and off for four decades, from *Sabrina* (1954) to the black dress of *Breakfast at Tiffany's* (1961). He also clothed Jacqueline Kennedy, Grace Kelly, the Duchess of Windsor, and Empress Farah Pahlavi, making Givenchy a byword for understated luxury.

He sold the house to LVMH in 1988 but remained its designer for seven more years. His influence persists in the enduring popularity of the little black dress, the separates-based wardrobe, and the label that still bears his name.

## Early Life & Background

Givenchy was born on 20 February 1927 in Beauvais, Oise. His father, Lucien Taffin de Givenchy, a marquis, died in 1930, and Hubert was raised by his mother, Béatrice Badin, and his maternal grandmother. His grandfather Jules Badin had directed the Beauvais and Gobelins tapestry manufactories, and the family's collections of textiles shaped his early taste.

In 1944 he moved to Paris to study at the École des Beaux-Arts. He trained under Jacques Fath from 1945, then worked for Robert Piguet and Lucien Lelong. From 1947 to 1951 he worked for Elsa Schiaparelli, eventually running her boutique on the Place Vendôme.

## Rise to Prominence

In February 1952, at age 24, Givenchy opened his house in the Plaine Monceau district. His debut collection of mix-and-match separates in inexpensive cotton shirting included the "Bettina blouse," named for model Bettina Graziani, and drew immediate press attention.

In 1953 he met Audrey Hepburn, who came to his studio to be dressed for *Sabrina*; he had expected Katharine Hepburn. The collaboration continued through *Funny Face* (1957), *Breakfast at Tiffany's* (1961), *Charade* (1963), and *How to Steal a Million* (1966). In 1957 he created the perfume L'Interdit for her.

That same year he met Balenciaga, who became his mentor. When Balenciaga closed his house in 1968, he sent his clients to Givenchy.

## Key Achievements

- **Luxury ready-to-wear:** In 1954 he became one of the first couturiers to launch a ready-to-wear line, Givenchy Université.
- **Silhouettes:** He introduced the sack dress in 1957 and the baby-doll and balloon shapes in 1958, alongside Balenciaga.
- **Clients:** He dressed Jacqueline Kennedy for the 1961 Paris state visit and for President Kennedy's funeral in 1963.
- **Expansion:** Parfums Givenchy launched in 1957; the Gentleman Givenchy menswear line followed in 1969.
- **Sale and retirement:** He sold the house to LVMH in 1988 for about $45 million and presented his final couture collection in July 1995.
- **Recognition:** Retrospectives were staged at the Fashion Institute of Technology in New York in 1982 and at the Palais Galliera in Paris in 1991.

## Controversies & Criticism

Givenchy was uncredited for Hepburn's *Sabrina* wardrobe; costume designer Edith Head accepted the 1955 Academy Award alone, a slight that fashion historians still debate. The 1957 sack dress was mocked by parts of the press for hiding the waist.

After his retirement he openly criticized LVMH's choice of successors, calling the appointments of John Galliano and Alexander McQueen a mistake and distancing himself from the house. Some critics also argued that his reputation rested heavily on Hepburn's image rather than on formal innovation.

## Legacy & Influence

Givenchy's minimalism anticipated the pared-down luxury of later decades. The house has been led since 1995 by Galliano, McQueen, Julien Macdonald, Riccardo Tisci, Clare Waight Keller, Matthew M. Williams, and Sarah Burton. He died on 10 March 2018, aged 91. A 2022 Christie's sale of his art and furniture collection realized more than €114 million.
