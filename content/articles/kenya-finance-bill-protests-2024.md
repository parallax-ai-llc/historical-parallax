## Summary
The 2024 Kenya Finance Bill protests were a wave of decentralized, largely youth-led demonstrations against tax increases proposed by President William Ruto's government, lasting from 18 June to early August 2024 [^1]. On 25 June protesters breached Parliament as lawmakers passed the bill, and police opened fire; the next day Ruto withdrew the legislation [^2]. Death tolls vary by source, from at least 50 reported by Kenya's national human rights commission in July 2024 to at least 73 documented by Human Rights Watch [^3][^2]. The movement, often called the "Gen Z" protests, reshaped Kenyan politics and triggered a long debate about police violence and enforced disappearances [^2][^6].

## Background
Kenya faced heavy public debt and fiscal pressure, and the Finance Bill 2024 aimed to raise revenue as part of a programme to lift the tax-to-GDP ratio, a goal linked to the government's International Monetary Fund arrangement [^1]. Proposed measures included 16 percent VAT on bread, a 2.5 percent motor vehicle tax, an eco-levy on imported goods, and higher excise duties on mobile money transfers [^1]. Opposition grew online under hashtags such as #RejectFinanceBill2024 and #OccupyParliament, organized mostly by people aged 18 to 35 without formal leaders [^1][^2].

## The Event
Demonstrations began in Nairobi on 18 June, when the bill was introduced in Parliament, and spread to other cities [^2]. On 25 June an estimated 3,000 to 4,000 protesters breached the Parliament compound as members of Parliament approved the bill; part of the building was set on fire and police fired on crowds, according to Human Rights Watch [^2]. On 26 June Ruto announced he would not sign the bill and withdrew it [^2]. Protests continued with demands for accountability and for Ruto's resignation, and some escalated into riots in early July [^1]. On 11 July Ruto dismissed almost his entire cabinet [^1].

## Response and Aftermath
After withdrawing the bill, Ruto ordered spending cuts in place of the lost revenue, according to Wikipedia [^1]. The Kenya National Commission on Human Rights (KNCHR) reported on 16 July 2024 that 50 people had died and 413 had been injured since the protests began [^3]. Human Rights Watch documented abductions, arbitrary arrests, torture, and killings of perceived protest leaders between June and August 2024, and noted that the interior ministry itself reported 132 missing protesters by September [^2]. The IMF later expressed regret over the bloodshed while defending the need for fiscal consolidation, according to Wikipedia [^1].

## Positive Perspectives
- Peaceful mass mobilization led to the withdrawal of the Finance Bill within about a week of its introduction [^2].
- The protests showed the organizing power of a young, digitally connected generation acting without party leadership [^1][^2].
- Ruto dissolved his cabinet and formed a broader government, and the state later allocated Sh2 billion to compensate victims of protest-related abuses since 2017, according to the Daily Nation [^1][^6].
- Kenyan courts intervened in 2025 to order the restoration of live broadcasts that authorities had cut during protest coverage, according to Al Jazeera [^4].
- Independent bodies such as the KNCHR and international groups documented abuses, creating a record for future accountability [^3][^2].

## Negative Perspectives
- Human Rights Watch documented at least 73 killings and warned the true number was likely higher [^2].
- The same report found that the Independent Policing Oversight Authority had not charged any police officer over protest killings [^2].
- Rights groups reported abductions and enforced disappearances of activists, some of whom were later found dead [^2][^4].
- The 2025 death in police custody of blogger Albert Ojwang, whose autopsy pointed to assault, revived anger at police abuses [^5].
- Interior minister Kipchumba Murkomen described the 2025 anniversary demonstrations as "terrorism disguised as dissent" [^4].

## Recent News
Anniversary protests on 25 June 2025 turned deadly; Amnesty Kenya and the KNCHR verified 16 deaths, mostly caused by police, while a medical-legal coalition reported 8 deaths and about 400 injured [^4][^7]. At the second anniversary on 25 June 2026, the Daily Nation described the uprising as one of the most consequential political events in modern Kenya and noted that young voters had become a decisive constituency ahead of the 2027 election [^6].
