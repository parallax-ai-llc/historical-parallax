## Summary

On 2 April 2025, which he called "Liberation Day," President Donald Trump announced a 10 percent baseline tariff on nearly all U.S. imports and higher "reciprocal" tariffs on dozens of trading partners, invoking the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) [^1]. The announcement set off one of the sharpest stock-market declines in decades and a tariff spiral with China [^2]. Lower courts ruled the tariffs unlawful in 2025, and on 20 February 2026 the U.S. Supreme Court held 6-3 in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* that IEEPA does not authorize tariffs [^3][^4]. The episode reshaped U.S. trade relations and tested the limits of presidential emergency power [^4].

## Background

Trump had campaigned on tariffs as a tool to cut the U.S. trade deficit and revive domestic manufacturing [^1]. In his Rose Garden address he signed Executive Order 14257, declaring a national emergency over what he called a "large and persistent" trade deficit [^1]. The reciprocal rates were calculated by dividing the U.S. goods deficit with each country by its exports to the United States and halving the result, a method economists criticized as having little relation to actual trade barriers [^1].

## The Event

The 10 percent baseline took effect on 5 April 2025 and the country-specific rates on 9 April; rates ranged up to 50 percent, with the EU at 20 percent, Japan 24 percent, India 26 percent and China 34 percent [^1]. Canada and Mexico were exempted from the new schedule [^1]. China retaliated with a 34 percent tariff on all U.S. goods [^2]. On 4 April the Dow fell 2,231 points, or 5.5 percent, the S&P 500 fell 5.97 percent, and the Nasdaq entered a bear market [^2]. As Treasury yields also jumped, Trump announced on 9 April a 90-day pause on the higher rates for every country except China, and the S&P 500 rose 9.52 percent that day [^1]. U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods escalated to 145 percent before a Geneva agreement on 12 May 2025 cut them to 30 percent, with China lowering its rate to 10 percent [^1].

## Response and Aftermath

Several trading partners negotiated deals, with Japan, South Korea and the EU accepting 15 percent rates in exchange for investment pledges, and new country rates took effect on 7 August 2025 [^1]. On 28 May 2025 the Court of International Trade ruled in *V.O.S. Selections v. United States* that Trump had exceeded his IEEPA authority, and the Federal Circuit upheld that ruling on 29 August 2025 while leaving the tariffs in place pending appeal [^1]. The Supreme Court heard arguments on 5 November 2025 and ruled on 20 February 2026, with Chief Justice John Roberts writing for the majority and Justices Thomas, Alito and Kavanaugh dissenting [^3][^4]. The administration then imposed a temporary universal tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 and opened Section 301 investigations [^3].

## Positive Perspectives

- Supporters, including the administration, argued the tariffs would raise revenue, boost domestic production and create American jobs [^1].
- The threat of tariffs brought Japan, South Korea and the EU to sign agreements that included large investment pledges in the United States [^1].
- The Supreme Court's ruling treated tariffs as part of Congress's Article I taxing power, reaffirming a check on presidential emergency authority [^4].
- U.S. Customs opened an automated refund system on 20 April 2026, and about $81 billion had been repaid by July 2026 [^3].

## Negative Perspectives

- Economists described the reciprocal formula as overly simplistic; the initial list even included tariffs on uninhabited territories such as the Heard and McDonald Islands [^1].
- The S&P 500 suffered its steepest four-day loss since the 1950s after the announcement, according to market analysts [^5].
- The courts found that the tariffs had been imposed without lawful authority, after about $166 billion had been collected from more than 330,000 importers [^3].
- More than 2,000 lawsuits sought fuller refunds, and the replacement tariffs drew their own legal challenges [^3].
- Country-specific rates were delayed several times before taking effect in August 2025, raising the average effective U.S. tariff rate above 17 percent [^1].

## Recent News

The Court of International Trade enjoined the Section 122 tariffs on 7 May 2026, a ruling later stayed on appeal [^3]. The Section 122 tariffs expired on 24 July 2026 and were replaced by Section 301 tariffs of 10 to 12.5 percent on 60 countries [^3].
