## Summary

Ferdinand de Saussure (1857–1913) was a Swiss linguist whose ideas reshaped the study of language in the twentieth century. He is widely regarded as a founder of modern linguistics and of semiotics, the general study of signs. His central move was to treat language as a structured system of relations rather than as a collection of historically evolving words.

Saussure published relatively little during his life, and his most influential work, the *Course in General Linguistics* (1916), was assembled after his death from students' lecture notes. Despite that indirect route, the book introduced distinctions such as langue and parole, signifier and signified, and synchronic and diachronic analysis that became standard vocabulary across the humanities.

His influence spread far beyond linguistics into anthropology, literary theory, psychoanalysis, and philosophy, where it underpinned the structuralist and post-structuralist movements of the 1950s through the 1980s.

## Early Life & Background

Saussure was born on 26 November 1857 in Geneva, Switzerland, into a prominent family of scientists and scholars. His father, Henri de Saussure, was an entomologist, and earlier ancestors included the naturalist Horace-Bénédict de Saussure.

He showed early aptitude for languages and, as a teenager, learned Sanskrit alongside Latin and Greek. After briefly studying chemistry and physics at the University of Geneva in 1875, he moved to the University of Leipzig in 1876, then the leading center of comparative Indo-European philology and home of the Neogrammarian school. He also spent time at the University of Berlin.

## Rise to Prominence

In 1878, at age 21, Saussure published his *Mémoire sur le système primitif des voyelles dans les langues indo-européennes*. The work proposed that Proto-Indo-European contained sound elements, later called laryngeals, that had disappeared but left traces in the vowel systems of daughter languages. The hypothesis was confirmed decades later, in 1927, when Jerzy Kuryłowicz identified reflexes of these sounds in Hittite.

He received his doctorate from Leipzig in 1880 with a thesis on the genitive absolute in Sanskrit. From 1881 to 1891 he taught Gothic, Old High German, and Indo-European comparative grammar at the École pratique des hautes études in Paris, where he trained a generation of French linguists, including Antoine Meillet.

In 1891 he returned to Geneva, becoming a professor of Sanskrit and Indo-European languages. Between 1907 and 1911 he delivered three courses in general linguistics that would form the basis of his posthumous reputation.

## Key Achievements

- **Laryngeal theory:** His 1878 *Mémoire* remains a landmark of Indo-European reconstruction.
- **Langue and parole:** He separated the shared social system of language from individual acts of speech, arguing that linguistics should study the former.
- **The linguistic sign:** He described the sign as a union of signifier (sound pattern) and signified (concept), and argued that the link between them is arbitrary.
- **Synchrony and diachrony:** He distinguished the study of a language at a single point in time from the study of its change across time, prioritizing the synchronic view.
- **Value and difference:** He argued that linguistic units derive meaning from their differences from other units within the system, not from intrinsic properties.

The *Course in General Linguistics* was edited by Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye and published in 1916.

## Controversies & Criticism

The *Course* is a reconstruction, not a text Saussure wrote, and scholars have long debated how faithfully it reflects his views. The 1996 discovery of his own manuscripts, published in 2002 as *Écrits de linguistique générale*, showed a more tentative thinker than the editors' confident synthesis suggested.

Critics such as Roman Jakobson and later generative linguists argued that Saussure's focus on synchrony and on the system neglected language change, universals, and the mental basis of grammar. His late research into hidden anagrams in Latin poetry, left unpublished, has been viewed by some as an eccentric pursuit.

## Legacy & Influence

Saussure's framework underlies the Prague School, Copenhagen School, and the structural anthropology of Claude Lévi-Strauss. Roland Barthes, Jacques Lacan, and Jacques Derrida each built on or reacted against his account of the sign. Semiotics as a discipline traces one of its two founding lines to him, alongside Charles Sanders Peirce.

He died on 22 February 1913 at Vufflens-le-Château, Switzerland, at age 55.
