## Summary

Chloé Zhao (born Zhao Ting, March 31, 1982) is a Chinese-born American film director, screenwriter, producer, and editor. She is best known for *Nomadland* (2020), which won the Golden Lion at Venice and three Academy Awards, including Best Picture and Best Director. With that win she became the second woman, and the first woman of color and first Asian woman, to receive the Academy Award for Best Director.

Zhao's work blends documentary and fiction. She casts non-professional actors playing versions of themselves, shoots on location in natural light, and builds loose scripts around real lives. Her first three features, set in the American West, established her as a leading voice of contemporary independent cinema before she moved to studio filmmaking with Marvel's *Eternals* (2021) and the literary adaptation *Hamnet* (2025).

## Early Life & Background

Zhao was born in Beijing. Her father, Zhao Yuji, was an executive at the state-owned steel company Shougang, and her stepmother is the comedic actress Song Dandan. As a teenager she drew manga and followed Western pop culture, and at age 14 she left China for Brighton College, a boarding school in England. She completed high school in Los Angeles.

She earned a bachelor's degree in political science from Mount Holyoke College in 2005, then worked odd jobs in New York before enrolling in the graduate film program at New York University's Tisch School of the Arts, where her instructors included Spike Lee.

## Rise to Prominence

Zhao's debut feature, *Songs My Brothers Taught Me* (2015), was shot on the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation in South Dakota with a largely Lakota cast. It premiered at the Sundance Film Festival in January 2015, screened in the Directors' Fortnight at Cannes, and earned an Independent Spirit Award nomination for Best First Feature.

Her second film, *The Rider* (2017), followed Brady Jandreau, a real rodeo cowboy recovering from a head injury, playing a fictionalized version of himself. It won the Art Cinema Award at Cannes Directors' Fortnight and was named Best Film by the National Society of Film Critics. The film brought her to the attention of Frances McDormand, who had optioned Jessica Bruder's nonfiction book *Nomadland* and asked Zhao to adapt it.

## Key Achievements

*Nomadland* premiered in September 2020, winning the Golden Lion at Venice and the People's Choice Award at Toronto. At the 93rd Academy Awards in April 2021 it won Best Picture, Best Director, and Best Actress. Zhao received four nominations that year, for picture, directing, adapted screenplay, and editing, the most ever for a woman in a single ceremony. She also won the Golden Globe, BAFTA, and Directors Guild of America awards for directing.

*Eternals*, released by Marvel Studios on November 5, 2021, was made on a budget of roughly $200 million and grossed about $402 million worldwide. *Hamnet*, adapted from Maggie O'Farrell's novel and starring Jessie Buckley and Paul Mescal, premiered at Telluride in August 2025, won the People's Choice Award at Toronto, and earned Buckley the Academy Award for Best Actress in March 2026. It also won the Golden Globe for Best Motion Picture – Drama.

## Controversies & Criticism

Zhao's Oscar win drew a backlash in China after a 2013 interview resurfaced in which she described her homeland as "a place where there are lies everywhere." Chinese state media suppressed news of the award, references to *Nomadland* were scrubbed from social media, and the film's planned Chinese release was cancelled. Hong Kong broadcaster TVB declined to air the ceremony for the first time since 1969.

Some critics argued that *Nomadland* softened Bruder's reporting on Amazon warehouse labor conditions. *Eternals* received the lowest critical scores of any Marvel film at the time, with a 47 percent Rotten Tomatoes rating, and was banned in Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and Qatar over a same-sex kiss.

## Legacy & Influence

Zhao's hybrid method, often compared to Terrence Malick and Italian neorealism, has become a reference point for a generation of independent filmmakers. Her long collaboration with cinematographer Joshua James Richards, her partner, defined a visual style of magic-hour landscapes and handheld intimacy. Her historic Oscar win widened the discussion about women and Asian directors in Hollywood, and her move between micro-budget independents and $200 million franchises remains a case study in how auteurs navigate the studio system.
