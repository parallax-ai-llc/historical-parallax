## Summary

Quincy Delight Jones Jr. (March 14, 1933 – November 3, 2024) was an American record producer, composer, arranger, and trumpeter whose career spanned more than seven decades. He worked across jazz, pop, film scoring, and television, and is best known for producing Michael Jackson's albums *Off the Wall* (1979), *Thriller* (1982), and *Bad* (1987), as well as the 1985 charity single "We Are the World".

Jones received 28 Grammy Awards from 80 nominations, among the most for any individual, along with an Emmy, a Tony, and two honorary Academy Awards. He was also a media executive who founded Qwest Records and *Vibe* magazine, and an early Black executive at a major American record label.

## Early Life & Background

Jones was born on the South Side of Chicago to Quincy Jones Sr., a carpenter, and Sarah Frances Jones, who was institutionalized with schizophrenia during his childhood. In 1943 the family relocated to Bremerton, Washington, and later to Seattle, where Jones attended Garfield High School. He took up the trumpet as a teenager and, at age 14, befriended a 16-year-old Ray Charles, who introduced him to arranging.

In 1951 Jones won a scholarship to Schillinger House in Boston, now Berklee College of Music. He left within a year to tour as a trumpeter and arranger with Lionel Hampton's band.

## Rise to Prominence

Through the 1950s Jones arranged for Count Basie, Dinah Washington, and Sarah Vaughan, and in 1956 he served as musical director for Dizzy Gillespie's State Department tour. In 1957 he moved to Paris, studied composition with Nadia Boulanger, and worked for Barclay Records. A 1959–60 European tour with his own big band left him deeply in debt.

In 1961 Mercury Records hired him and later named him vice president, a first for a Black executive at a major U.S. label. He produced Lesley Gore's "It's My Party", a number one hit in 1963. He then moved into film scoring with *The Pawnbroker* (1964), *In Cold Blood* (1967), and *In the Heat of the Night* (1967), and composed television themes for *Ironside* and *Sanford and Son*. In 1974 he survived two operations for a brain aneurysm.

## Key Achievements

Jones met Michael Jackson while scoring *The Wiz* (1978). Their collaboration on *Thriller* produced the best-selling album in history, with estimated sales above 70 million copies, and the album won eight Grammys in 1984. In 1985 he produced "We Are the World" for USA for Africa, which raised more than $60 million for famine relief, and co-produced the film *The Color Purple*, which earned 11 Oscar nominations.

He founded Qwest Records in 1980, produced Frank Sinatra's *L.A. Is My Lady* (1984), and won the Grammy for Album of the Year for his own *Back on the Block* (1989). He was executive producer of *The Fresh Prince of Bel-Air* (1990–96) and co-founded *Vibe* in 1993. Honors include the Grammy Legend Award (1992), the Jean Hersholt Humanitarian Award (1995), Kennedy Center Honors (2001), the National Medal of Arts (2010), and an Honorary Academy Award presented in November 2024.

## Controversies & Criticism

In February 2018 Jones gave interviews to *GQ* and *Vulture* that drew widespread criticism. He called the Beatles "the worst musicians in the world", claimed Michael Jackson had lifted parts of "Billie Jean" from Donna Summer's "State of Independence", and made unverified claims about Marlon Brando and Richard Pryor. After what he described as a "family intervention" by his daughters, he issued a public apology.

Jones also sued the Michael Jackson estate over royalties from posthumous projects. A jury awarded him $9.4 million in 2017, but a California appeals court reduced the award to about $6.9 million in 2020. Some critics argued the suit strained his image as Jackson's mentor.

## Legacy & Influence

Jones died at his home in Bel Air, Los Angeles, on November 3, 2024, at age 91. His work as an arranger helped define the sound of postwar big-band jazz, and his productions with Jackson set commercial and technical benchmarks for pop recording. He was among the first Black composers to score major Hollywood films and helped open executive roles in the music industry to Black professionals.

The 2018 Netflix documentary *Quincy*, co-directed by his daughter Rashida Jones, chronicled his career. The Quincy Jones Foundation continued his philanthropic work in music education and global health.
