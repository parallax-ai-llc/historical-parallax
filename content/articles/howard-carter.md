## Summary

Howard Carter (9 May 1874 – 2 March 1939) was a British archaeologist and Egyptologist best known for discovering the tomb of the pharaoh Tutankhamun in the Valley of the Kings in November 1922. The tomb, catalogued as KV62, was the most intact royal burial ever found in Egypt and contained more than 5,000 objects.

Carter spent over three decades in Egypt before the discovery, working as a draughtsman, excavator, and government antiquities inspector. The Tutankhamun find, funded by George Herbert, 5th Earl of Carnarvon, made him internationally famous and set off a global wave of interest in ancient Egypt.

His careful, decade-long clearance of the tomb established new standards for recording and conserving excavated material, even as his handling of press, politics, and artefacts drew lasting criticism.

## Early Life & Background

Carter was born in Kensington, London, the youngest of eleven children of Samuel John Carter, an animal painter, and Martha Joyce Carter. Because of poor health he was raised largely by relatives in Swaffham, Norfolk, and received little formal schooling. His father trained him in drawing, a skill that determined his career.

In 1891, aged 17, he was sent to Egypt by the Egypt Exploration Fund to copy tomb paintings at Beni Hasan under Percy Newberry. In 1892 he worked briefly with Flinders Petrie at Amarna, and from 1893 to 1899 he recorded the reliefs of Hatshepsut's temple at Deir el-Bahari for Édouard Naville.

## Rise to Prominence

In 1899 Gaston Maspero appointed Carter Chief Inspector of Antiquities for Upper Egypt, where he supervised excavations and installed electric lighting in tombs at Thebes. He transferred to Lower Egypt in 1904. In 1905, after a confrontation at Saqqara between Egyptian site guards and French tourists, Carter refused to apologise to the visitors and resigned.

He spent several years selling watercolours and dealing in antiquities. In 1907 Maspero introduced him to Lord Carnarvon, who hired him to direct excavations in the Theban necropolis. In 1914 Carnarvon secured the concession for the Valley of the Kings, though the First World War delayed systematic work until 1917.

## Key Achievements

After five largely unproductive seasons, Carnarvon agreed to fund one final campaign. On 4 November 1922 Carter's workmen uncovered a step cut into the bedrock. On 26 November he opened a hole in the sealed doorway and, asked whether he could see anything, reportedly replied, "Yes, wonderful things." The burial chamber was formally opened on 16 February 1923.

Clearing the tomb took until 1932. Carter, working with conservator Alfred Lucas and photographer Harry Burton, numbered, photographed, and documented every object before removal. He published a three-volume popular account, *The Tomb of Tut.ankh.Amen*, between 1923 and 1933. Yale University awarded him an honorary doctorate in 1924.

## Controversies & Criticism

Carnarvon sold exclusive coverage to *The Times* in January 1923, angering Egyptian and rival foreign newspapers. Relations with the Egyptian Antiquities Service under Pierre Lacau deteriorated, and in February 1924 Carter locked the tomb during a dispute over access. The government cancelled the concession, and work resumed only in January 1925 under stricter terms.

Later research indicated that Carter, Carnarvon, and Lady Evelyn Herbert entered the burial chamber unofficially before the formal opening. Several objects from the tomb were traced to Carter's estate and to museums abroad; the Metropolitan Museum of Art returned items to Egypt in 2010. The British government never awarded him an honour.

## Legacy & Influence

Carter died of Hodgkin's lymphoma in London on 2 March 1939, aged 64, and was buried at Putney Vale Cemetery. Only a handful of mourners attended.

His discovery produced the phenomenon known as "Tutmania," influencing 1920s design and fashion. Touring exhibitions of the treasures in 1972 and 2005 drew millions of visitors, and the full collection is now displayed at the Grand Egyptian Museum in Giza. His excavation records, held at the Griffith Institute in Oxford, remain a primary resource for Egyptologists.
