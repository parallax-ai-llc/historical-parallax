## Summary

Marie Jean Antoine Nicolas de Caritat, Marquis de Condorcet (17 September 1743 – 29 March 1794), was a French mathematician, philosopher, and political theorist of the late Enlightenment. He was the only major philosophe to take an active role in the French Revolution, serving in both the Legislative Assembly and the National Convention.

Condorcet is best remembered for founding the mathematical study of collective decision-making. His 1785 essay on the probability of majority decisions introduced the Condorcet jury theorem, the Condorcet paradox, and the pairwise voting method that still carries his name. These ideas underpin modern social choice theory.

He was also an early advocate of causes that were radical for his era, including the abolition of slavery, equal political rights for women, and free universal public education. His final work, the *Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind*, written while in hiding from the Jacobin government, became a foundational statement of the idea of human progress.

## Early Life & Background

Condorcet was born in Ribemont, Picardy, into a family of minor nobility. His father, a cavalry officer, died when he was an infant, and he was raised by a devout mother. He was educated at the Jesuit college in Reims and then at the Collège de Navarre in Paris, where he showed exceptional ability in mathematics.

In 1765 he published his *Essai sur le calcul intégral*, which brought him to the attention of Jean le Rond d'Alembert. Under d'Alembert's patronage he entered Parisian intellectual circles and was elected to the Académie des sciences in 1769, at the age of 25.

## Rise to Prominence

Condorcet became permanent secretary of the Académie des sciences in 1776 and was elected to the Académie française in 1782. Through his friendship with the economist Anne Robert Jacques Turgot, he was appointed Inspector General of the Mint in 1775, a post he held until 1791.

His 1785 *Essai sur l'application de l'analyse à la probabilité des décisions rendues à la pluralité des voix* applied probability theory to voting and juries. In 1786 he married Sophie de Grouchy, whose salon became a gathering place for reformers. Their daughter Eliza was born in 1790.

When the Revolution began, Condorcet embraced it. He was elected to the Paris municipal government in 1790, to the Legislative Assembly in 1791, and to the National Convention in 1792.

## Key Achievements

- **Social choice theory:** The Condorcet method compares candidates in pairwise contests; the Condorcet paradox showed that majority preferences can be cyclical and intransitive.
- **Jury theorem:** He proved that if each voter is more likely than not to be correct, the probability of a correct majority decision rises as the group grows.
- **Abolitionism:** His 1781 *Réflexions sur l'esclavage des nègres*, published under a pseudonym, condemned slavery, and he later joined the Société des Amis des Noirs.
- **Women's rights:** His 1790 essay *On the Admission of Women to the Rights of Citizenship* argued that excluding women from political rights violated natural equality.
- **Public education:** His April 1792 report to the Legislative Assembly proposed a secular, free, and universal school system.
- **Constitutional design:** He was the principal author of the Girondin constitutional draft presented in February 1793.

## Controversies & Criticism

Condorcet voted against the execution of Louis XVI, a position that placed him at odds with the Montagnards. After the Convention adopted a rival constitution in June 1793, he published a pamphlet attacking it. On 8 July 1793 he was denounced and an arrest warrant was issued.

Contemporaries criticized him as politically indecisive, and later critics, notably Thomas Malthus in his 1798 *Essay on the Principle of Population*, attacked the optimism of his theory of indefinite progress. Some historians also note the tension between his commitment to reason and the Terror that consumed his allies.

## Legacy & Influence

Condorcet spent roughly eight months hiding in the Paris home of Madame Vernet, where he wrote the *Esquisse*. He fled on 25 March 1794, was arrested two days later at Clamart, and died in a cell at Bourg-la-Reine on 29 March. Whether he died of exhaustion or by poison remains unresolved.

The *Esquisse* was published posthumously in 1795. His voting theory was revived by Kenneth Arrow and Duncan Black in the twentieth century and remains central to political science and economics. In 1989 France symbolically interred him in the Panthéon.
