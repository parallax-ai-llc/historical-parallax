## Summary

Daisy Lee Gatson Bates (November 11, 1914 – November 4, 1999) was an American civil rights activist, newspaper publisher, and the central organizer of the 1957 desegregation of Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas. As president of the Arkansas State Conference of NAACP branches, she recruited, counseled, and protected the nine Black students known as the Little Rock Nine during the most closely watched school integration crisis of the era.

With her husband L.C. Bates, she co-published the *Arkansas State Press*, a weekly Black newspaper that documented police violence and segregation from 1941 until advertiser boycotts closed it in 1959. Her 1962 memoir, *The Long Shadow of Little Rock*, remains a primary source on the crisis, and she was the only woman to speak from the podium at the 1963 March on Washington.

## Early Life & Background

Bates was born in Huttig, a sawmill town in southern Arkansas. When she was an infant, her mother was killed; Bates later wrote that three white men had raped and murdered her, and that no one was ever charged. Her father left, and she was raised by family friends Orlee and Susie Smith. She attended segregated schools in Huttig and left before finishing high school.

In 1942 she married Lucious Christopher "L.C." Bates, an insurance salesman and former journalist. The couple had already moved to Little Rock and, in 1941, launched the *Arkansas State Press*, which quickly became the state's leading Black newspaper with a circulation that reached roughly 10,000.

## Rise to Prominence

Bates was elected president of the Arkansas State Conference of NAACP branches in 1952. After the Supreme Court's *Brown v. Board of Education* ruling in 1954, she pressed the Little Rock school board to move faster on integration and used the newspaper to publicize its delays.

When Central High School was scheduled to admit Black students in September 1957, Bates became the students' organizer and spokesperson. On September 4, 1957, Governor Orval Faubus ordered the Arkansas National Guard to block their entry. President Dwight Eisenhower federalized the Guard and dispatched the 101st Airborne Division, and the nine students entered on September 25, 1957. Throughout the school year Bates's home served as their meeting point; rocks were thrown through its windows and crosses were burned on the lawn.

## Key Achievements

- Guided the Little Rock Nine through the 1957–58 school year, including daily coordination with federal authorities and the press.
- Shared the NAACP's Spingarn Medal with the Little Rock Nine in 1958.
- Won *Bates v. Little Rock* (1960), in which the Supreme Court unanimously overturned her 1957 conviction for refusing to surrender NAACP membership lists.
- Published *The Long Shadow of Little Rock* in 1962, with a foreword by Eleanor Roosevelt; it received an American Book Award in 1988.
- Spoke at the March on Washington on August 28, 1963, delivering the "Tribute to Negro Women Fighters for Freedom."
- Later worked on Democratic National Committee voter registration and Johnson-era anti-poverty programs, then led a self-help community project in Mitchellville, Arkansas, after a 1965 stroke.
- Revived the *Arkansas State Press* in 1984 and sold it in 1987.

## Controversies & Criticism

Bates was criticized for the events of September 4, 1957, when Elizabeth Eckford, whose family had no telephone, was not told of the group's meeting point and faced a hostile mob alone. Some parents of the Nine and NAACP colleagues, including national executive Roy Wilkins, considered her too eager for publicity, and she clashed with local ministers over strategy. Historians have also questioned details of her account of her mother's death, which rests on her own memoir. Her later financial difficulties and reliance on political patronage drew comment in Arkansas.

## Legacy & Influence

Arkansas has observed Daisy Gatson Bates Day on the third Monday of February since 2001, and her Little Rock home was designated a National Historic Landmark the same year. A Little Rock elementary school bears her name. In 2024 a statue of Bates was installed in the U.S. Capitol's National Statuary Hall, representing Arkansas alongside Johnny Cash. Her role in Little Rock made her one of the few women publicly recognized as a strategic leader in the 1950s civil rights movement.
