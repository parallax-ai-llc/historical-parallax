## Summary

Dame Kathleen Mary Kenyon (5 January 1906 – 24 August 1978) was a British archaeologist best known for her excavations at Jericho (1952–1958) and Jerusalem (1961–1967). She was among the most influential Near Eastern archaeologists of the 20th century and a leading figure in bringing rigorous stratigraphic excavation to the Levant.

Her work at Jericho pushed the known history of settled urban life back to roughly 8000 BC, revealing a Neolithic stone tower and walls that predate pottery. She also concluded that no fortified city stood at Jericho during the period traditionally assigned to the biblical conquest, a finding that reshaped debates on archaeology and the Bible.

Kenyon refined the excavation technique of Mortimer Wheeler into what is now called the Wheeler-Kenyon method. She trained generations of field archaeologists, served as Principal of St Hugh's College, Oxford, and was made a Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire in 1973.

## Early Life & Background

Kenyon was born in London, the elder daughter of Sir Frederic Kenyon, a biblical scholar who served as Director of the British Museum from 1909 to 1931. She grew up in the museum's official residence and attended St Paul's Girls' School before reading history at Somerville College, Oxford, graduating in 1929. At Oxford she became the first woman president of the Oxford University Archaeological Society.

Her first fieldwork came in 1929, when she joined Gertrude Caton-Thompson's expedition to Great Zimbabwe as a photographer. Between 1930 and 1935 she worked at the Roman city of Verulamium (St Albans) under Mortimer and Tessa Wheeler, directing the excavation of the Roman theatre and absorbing Wheeler's grid-and-section technique.

## Rise to Prominence

From 1931 to 1934 Kenyon worked at Samaria in Mandatory Palestine with John and Grace Crowfoot, where she introduced careful stratigraphic recording to a region where it was still unusual. In 1934 she helped Wheeler found the Institute of Archaeology at the University of London, serving as its Secretary and then Acting Director during the Second World War (1942–1946). She also commanded a Red Cross division in Hammersmith during the war. From 1948 to 1962 she lectured in Palestinian archaeology at the Institute.

In 1951 she became Honorary Director of the British School of Archaeology in Jerusalem, a position she held until 1966. This gave her the platform for her two landmark excavations.

## Key Achievements

- **Jericho (Tell es-Sultan), 1952–1958:** Kenyon uncovered a Pre-Pottery Neolithic settlement with a stone tower about 8.5 metres high and a defensive wall, dated to around 8000 BC. She also found plastered human skulls with shell eyes. She argued that the Middle Bronze Age city was destroyed around 1550 BC and that no walled town existed in the late 15th or 13th century BC, contradicting John Garstang's earlier claims.
- **Jerusalem, 1961–1967:** Her excavations on the eastern slope of the City of David traced the Bronze and Iron Age town and showed that the earliest city lay outside the later walled area.
- **Method:** The Wheeler-Kenyon method, using 5-metre squares separated by standing balks, became standard practice in Levantine archaeology.
- **Publications:** *Beginning in Archaeology* (1952), *Digging Up Jericho* (1957), *Archaeology in the Holy Land* (1960), and *Digging Up Jerusalem* (1974).
- **Honours:** CBE (1954), Fellow of the British Academy (1955), DBE (1973), and the Principalship of St Hugh's College, Oxford (1962–1973).

## Controversies & Criticism

Kenyon was repeatedly criticised for slow publication. Only two of the five final Jericho volumes appeared in her lifetime, and the remainder were completed by colleagues between 1981 and 1983. Much of the Jerusalem material was published posthumously by others, limiting scholars' ability to check her interpretations.

Her narrow-trench method, while stratigraphically precise, exposed relatively little architecture, and some critics argued it produced sound sequences but poor understanding of whole buildings. Her Jericho chronology was challenged in 1990 by Bryant Wood, who argued the pottery supported a destruction around 1400 BC; most archaeologists have not accepted his redating. Israeli archaeologists, including Yohanan Aharoni and Ruth Amiran, disputed her dating of Samaria pottery, and later excavations by Yigal Shiloh revised several of her Jerusalem conclusions. Some contemporaries also noted her cool relations with Israeli scholarship.

## Legacy & Influence

Kenyon's Jericho results remain foundational for the study of early Neolithic settlement, and her stratigraphic approach shaped fieldwork across the Middle East. The British School of Archaeology in Jerusalem was renamed the Kenyon Institute in her honour in 2003. She is widely regarded as one of the most important women in the history of archaeology and a key figure in professionalising the discipline.
