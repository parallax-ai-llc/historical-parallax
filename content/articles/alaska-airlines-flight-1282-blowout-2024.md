## Summary
On 5 January 2024, a door plug blew out of the fuselage of Alaska Airlines Flight 1282, a nearly new Boeing 737 MAX 9, while it climbed through about 16,000 feet after departing Portland, Oregon [^1]. The cabin rapidly decompressed, but the aircraft returned safely to Portland and no one was killed [^1]. Investigators found that four bolts meant to hold the plug in place had not been reinstalled at Boeing's factory [^2]. The accident triggered a grounding, a cap on Boeing's production, and a renewed federal criminal review of the company [^3][^4].

## Background
The 737 MAX family had already been grounded worldwide in 2019 after two fatal crashes, and Boeing had entered a deferred prosecution agreement with the US Justice Department in 2021 [^4]. Some 737 MAX 9 configurations use a "door plug" to seal an unused mid-cabin emergency exit [^1]. The accident aircraft, registration N704AL, was about two months old at the time of the accident [^1].

## The Event
Flight 1282 departed Portland International Airport for Ontario, California, with 171 passengers and 6 crew [^1]. At about 5:07 p.m. PST, roughly six minutes after takeoff, the left mid-cabin door plug separated, leaving a hole in the side of the aircraft [^1][^2]. Oxygen masks deployed and loose items were sucked out over the Portland suburbs; by chance, seat 26A, immediately next to the opening, was unoccupied [^1]. The crew returned to Portland and landed safely [^1]. The NTSB final report counted one flight attendant and seven passengers with minor injuries, while some accounts list fewer injuries [^2][^1].

## Response and Aftermath
The FAA ordered the temporary grounding of 171 Boeing 737 MAX 9 aircraft with door plugs for inspection [^3]. Flights resumed in late January after approved inspections [^1]. The FAA also told Boeing it would not approve any increase in 737 MAX production until quality-control problems were resolved [^3]. The NTSB's preliminary report in February 2024 concluded that the four retention bolts were missing when the plug departed [^1]. In May 2024, the Justice Department told a federal court that Boeing had breached its 2021 deferred prosecution agreement, citing failures in its compliance program after the door plug incident and other safety lapses [^4]. Boeing paid Alaska Airlines about $160 million in compensation for losses during the grounding, according to Wikipedia [^1].

## Positive Perspectives
- The flight crew managed an explosive decompression and returned the aircraft safely, with no fatalities [^1].
- Regulators grounded the affected fleet within a day and required inspections before return to service [^3].
- The FAA's production cap signalled a tougher oversight stance toward Boeing [^3].
- The NTSB's final report issued wide-ranging recommendations, including door plug redesign and retrofits across in-service 737s [^2][^5].

## Negative Perspectives
- The NTSB found that Boeing's safety and quality systems failed to catch the missing bolts and did not properly document work on the door plug [^2].
- The board also cited ineffective FAA oversight as a contributing factor [^5].
- The Justice Department concluded in 2024 that Boeing had violated its 2021 agreement, reviving questions about corporate accountability after the earlier fatal crashes [^4].
- In November 2025 a federal judge approved dismissal of the crash-related criminal case under a non-prosecution deal, while saying the agreement "fails to secure the necessary accountability," according to Al Jazeera [^6].

## Recent News
The NTSB adopted its final report on 24 June 2025, describing a systemic failure of Boeing's manufacturing processes and FAA oversight [^1][^5]. On 6 November 2025 Judge Reed O'Connor granted the Justice Department's request to dismiss the related criminal case against Boeing under a non-prosecution agreement [^6].
