## Summary

Hurricane Melissa was a Category 5 Atlantic hurricane. It made landfall in southwestern Jamaica on 28 October 2025 with sustained winds of about 185 mph (295 km/h), making it the strongest hurricane on record to strike the island [^1][^2]. The storm went on to hit eastern Cuba and caused deadly flooding in Haiti. The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) says it killed more than 90 people across the Caribbean [^3]. The World Bank and the Inter-American Development Bank estimated physical damage in Jamaica alone at US$8.8 billion, about 41 percent of the country's 2024 GDP [^4].

## Background

Melissa formed as a tropical storm in the central Caribbean on 21 October 2025 and moved slowly for several days. It then intensified rapidly over very warm water south of Jamaica [^1]. Wikipedia's account, based on National Hurricane Center (NHC) data, gives a peak of 190 mph and a minimum pressure of 892 mbar. That ties Hurricane Allen's wind record and is among the lowest pressures recorded for an Atlantic storm [^1]. The storm's slow movement brought days of heavy rain to Haiti and the Dominican Republic before landfall [^5].

## The Event

Melissa came ashore near New Hope, Westmoreland Parish, around 1 p.m. Eastern time on 28 October 2025 as a Category 5 storm with sustained winds of about 185 mph. NPR member station WLRN reported that it was the strongest Atlantic hurricane on record to make landfall [^2]. The storm surge and winds devastated western Jamaica. UN Resident Coordinator Dennis Zulu described destruction "never seen before" on the island, and about 70 percent of electricity customers lost power [^5]. Melissa made a second landfall early on 29 October near Chivirico in Santiago de Cuba province as a Category 3 hurricane [^1]. It then crossed the Bahamas and became extratropical on 31 October [^1].

Haiti was not hit directly but suffered severe flooding. As of 31 October, Al Jazeera reported 30 deaths and 20 missing in Haiti, 19 deaths in Jamaica, and one in the Dominican Republic [^5]. Later tallies were higher. Wikipedia lists 45 deaths in Jamaica, 43 in Haiti, 4 in the Dominican Republic, and 1 in Cuba, with dozens still missing [^1].

## Response and Aftermath

Jamaica received the full US$150 million payout from a World Bank catastrophe bond. An independent calculation agent found that the storm's central pressure and track met the bond's pre-agreed triggers [^6]. On 19 November 2025 the World Bank and IDB estimated the island's physical damage at US$8.8 billion: 41 percent to homes, 33 percent to infrastructure, 21 percent to non-residential buildings, and 5 percent to agriculture [^4]. Lenders including the IMF, World Bank, and regional development banks later announced a combined support package of about US$6.7 billion, according to Wikipedia [^1]. The United States sent disaster assistance teams to Jamaica, Haiti, the Bahamas, and Cuba [^5].

## Positive Perspectives

- Jamaica's catastrophe bond paid out in full within weeks, giving the government quick liquidity for recovery [^6].
- The rapid damage assessment by the World Bank and IDB gave Jamaica a detailed early estimate of losses by sector to guide reconstruction [^4].
- International lenders and donors announced multi-billion-dollar financing for recovery, and governments and companies sent emergency aid [^1][^5].
- The WMO retired the name Melissa in March 2026, which acknowledges the scale of the disaster and ensures the name will not be reused [^3].

## Negative Perspectives

- Haiti, which was spared a direct hit, still recorded one of the highest death tolls because of floods. This exposed how vulnerable the country remains to heavy rain [^5].
- Damage in Jamaica equal to about 41 percent of GDP is a severe economic shock for a small island state, and broader economic losses are expected to be higher still [^4].
- Death toll figures varied for months as missing-person cases were resolved. Published totals range from "more than 90" to the mid-90s [^1][^3].
- The storm's rapid strengthening over very warm water, and its record intensity, renewed concern about how prepared Caribbean states are for stronger storms [^1][^2].

## Recent News

On 4 March 2026 the WMO Hurricane Committee retired the name Melissa from the Atlantic list and chose Molly as its replacement. Melissa became the 100th Atlantic name to be retired [^3].
