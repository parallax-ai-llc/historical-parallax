## Summary

Fannie Lou Hamer (October 6, 1917 – March 14, 1977) was an American civil rights and voting rights activist from the Mississippi Delta. A sharecropper with a sixth-grade education, she became one of the most recognizable grassroots leaders of the 1960s movement, known for the phrase "I'm sick and tired of being sick and tired."

Hamer co-founded the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP) in 1964 and delivered a nationally televised testimony at that year's Democratic National Convention describing the beatings and intimidation she endured for trying to register to vote. Her account helped build public support for the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

She also organized for economic self-sufficiency, founding the Freedom Farm Cooperative in 1969, and remained a voice for poor Black Southerners until her death from cancer at age 59.

## Early Life & Background

Hamer was born Fannie Lou Townsend in Montgomery County, Mississippi, the youngest of 20 children of sharecroppers James Lee and Lou Ella Townsend. The family moved to Sunflower County in 1919, and she began picking cotton around age six. She left school at 12 to work full-time, though she had learned to read and write.

In 1945 she married Perry "Pap" Hamer, and the couple worked on the W. D. Marlow plantation near Ruleville for 18 years. In 1961, while undergoing surgery to remove a uterine tumor, she was given a hysterectomy without her consent, a forced sterilization she later called a "Mississippi appendectomy." The Hamers adopted two daughters.

## Rise to Prominence

On August 31, 1962, after attending a Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) meeting, Hamer traveled with 17 others to Indianola to register to vote. She failed the literacy test, and the plantation owner fired and evicted her that same day. She passed the test on her third attempt in January 1963.

On June 9, 1963, returning from a citizenship training workshop, Hamer and companions were arrested in Winona, Mississippi. Police ordered two Black inmates to beat her with a blackjack, leaving her with permanent kidney damage, a blood clot behind one eye, and a limp. The federal government prosecuted the officers, who were acquitted by an all-white jury in December 1963.

She became a SNCC field secretary and ran for Congress in 1964. That summer she helped organize the Freedom Summer voter registration drive and co-founded the MFDP to challenge Mississippi's all-white delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City.

## Key Achievements

- **1964 DNC testimony (August 22):** Her televised statement to the Credentials Committee was cut short when President Lyndon Johnson called an impromptu press conference, but networks replayed it in full that evening. The MFDP rejected a compromise offering two at-large seats.
- **Voting Rights Act of 1965:** Her testimony and MFDP organizing are widely credited with strengthening the political case for the law.
- **Freedom Farm Cooperative (1969):** Hamer bought 40 acres, later expanded to roughly 640, to let poor families grow food and own land. It also ran a "pig bank" that distributed livestock.
- **National Women's Political Caucus (1971):** She was a founding member.
- **Legal victories:** Her 1969 lawsuit *Hamer v. Campbell* helped reverse a Sunflower County election and established precedent for enforcing Black voting rights. She also helped desegregate Mississippi's Democratic delegation in 1968.

## Controversies & Criticism

Hamer's uncompromising stance at the 1964 convention drew criticism from national civil rights leaders and liberal allies, including Roy Wilkins and Hubert Humphrey, who urged acceptance of the two-seat compromise. Her retort, "We didn't come all this way for no two seats," became famous but strained relations with the NAACP and the Johnson administration.

She opposed abortion and criticized some middle-class feminists, positions that placed her at odds with parts of the women's movement she helped organize. The Freedom Farm Cooperative struggled financially and collapsed by the mid-1970s, and some historians note its management difficulties alongside its achievements.

## Legacy & Influence

Hamer died in Mound Bayou, Mississippi, on March 14, 1977, of complications from breast cancer and hypertension. Her gravestone bears her signature phrase. She was inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 1993, and a statue was dedicated in Ruleville in 2012. The Fannie Lou Hamer National Statue Building Fund and numerous schools, streets, and scholarships bear her name. Her testimony remains a standard text in civil rights education, and her model of grassroots, economically grounded organizing continues to influence voting rights advocates.
