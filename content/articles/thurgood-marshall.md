## Summary

Thurgood Marshall (July 2, 1908 – January 24, 1993) was an American lawyer and jurist who served as the first African American justice of the United States Supreme Court from 1967 to 1991. Before joining the bench, he was the leading civil rights litigator of his era, arguing 32 cases before the Supreme Court as counsel for the NAACP and winning 29 of them.

His most consequential victory was Brown v. Board of Education (1954), in which a unanimous Court held that racial segregation in public schools violated the Fourteenth Amendment. That decision dismantled the legal foundation of "separate but equal" established in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) and became the cornerstone of the modern civil rights movement.

On the Court, Marshall was a consistent liberal voice, defending individual rights, affirmative action, and the rights of criminal defendants, and opposing the death penalty in every capital case he heard.

## Early Life & Background

Marshall was born in Baltimore, Maryland, to William Marshall, a railroad porter and later a club steward, and Norma Williams Marshall, a schoolteacher. He graduated from Frederick Douglass High School in 1925 and from Lincoln University in Pennsylvania in 1930, where his classmates included the poet Langston Hughes.

The University of Maryland School of Law refused to admit Black students, so Marshall enrolled at Howard University School of Law in Washington, D.C. There he studied under Dean Charles Hamilton Houston, who trained students to use litigation as a weapon against segregation. Marshall graduated first in his class in 1933.

## Rise to Prominence

In 1935, Marshall and Houston won Murray v. Pearson, forcing the University of Maryland to admit a Black applicant to the law school that had rejected Marshall. He joined the NAACP national staff in 1936, became its chief counsel in 1938, and in 1940 founded the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, which he led for more than two decades.

Over the following years he won a series of landmark cases: Chambers v. Florida (1940) on coerced confessions, Smith v. Allwright (1944) striking down all-white primaries, Shelley v. Kraemer (1948) barring enforcement of racially restrictive covenants, and Sweatt v. Painter (1950) on graduate school segregation. Brown v. Board of Education in 1954 capped this strategy.

President John F. Kennedy appointed Marshall to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit in 1961. Southern senators delayed his confirmation for nearly a year. In 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson named him Solicitor General, the first African American to hold that office; he won 14 of the 19 cases he argued.

## Key Achievements

Johnson nominated Marshall to the Supreme Court on June 13, 1967. The Senate confirmed him on August 30 by a vote of 69 to 11, and he took his seat on October 2, 1967. He served 24 years.

Notable opinions include Stanley v. Georgia (1969), protecting private possession of obscene material, and his concurrence in Furman v. Georgia (1972), which temporarily halted executions nationwide. His dissents in San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez (1973) and Milliken v. Bradley (1974) argued for equal educational funding and for desegregation remedies that crossed district lines.

He received the NAACP's Spingarn Medal in 1946, the Liberty Medal in 1992, and a posthumous Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1993.

## Controversies & Criticism

Conservatives criticized Marshall as a judicial activist who read his policy preferences into the Constitution. His 1987 speech marking the Constitution's bicentennial, which called the original document "defective from the start," drew sharp objections.

Records released after his death showed that in the 1950s he had shared information with the FBI about suspected communist influence within the NAACP, which some historians view as a compromise of the organization's independence. More militant activists, including Malcolm X, had earlier faulted his reliance on the courts as too gradual. In his final years, critics questioned whether he delegated too much work to his law clerks.

## Legacy & Influence

Marshall retired on October 1, 1991, and was succeeded by Clarence Thomas. He died of heart failure in Bethesda, Maryland, at age 84, and was buried at Arlington National Cemetery.

His litigation strategy, which attacked segregation case by case through the courts, remains a model for public interest law. Baltimore's airport was renamed Baltimore/Washington International Thurgood Marshall Airport in 2005, and the University of Maryland's law library bears his name. His career is dramatized in the film *Marshall* (2017).
