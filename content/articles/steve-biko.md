## Summary

Bantu Stephen Biko (18 December 1946 – 12 September 1977) was a South African anti-apartheid activist, writer, and the principal founder of the Black Consciousness Movement. Working in the late 1960s and 1970s, when the African National Congress and Pan Africanist Congress were banned and their leaders imprisoned or exiled, Biko rebuilt organised Black political activity inside South Africa around the idea that psychological liberation had to precede political liberation.

Biko died in police custody at the age of 30 after sustaining severe head injuries during interrogation in Port Elizabeth. His death provoked international outrage, contributed to the United Nations Security Council arms embargo against South Africa in November 1977, and made him one of the most widely recognised martyrs of the anti-apartheid struggle. His collected writings, published as *I Write What I Like* (1978), remain a foundational text of Black political thought in Africa and beyond.

## Early Life & Background

Biko was born in Tarkastad, in the Eastern Cape, the third of four children of Mzingaye Mathew Biko, a government clerk, and Alice Nokuzola Biko, a domestic worker. The family moved to Ginsberg township outside King William's Town, where Biko grew up. His father died in 1950, when Biko was four.

He attended Lovedale Institute in 1963 but was expelled after a few months following his brother's arrest for suspected Poqo activity. He then studied at St. Francis College, a Catholic boarding school in Mariannhill, Natal, matriculating in 1965. In 1966 he enrolled in the medical school of the University of Natal's "Non-European" section in Durban, where he became active in the multiracial National Union of South African Students (NUSAS).

## Rise to Prominence

Biko grew frustrated with NUSAS, which he saw as dominated by white liberals who set the agenda for Black members. In 1968 he led the formation of the South African Students' Organisation (SASO), an all-Black student body, and was elected its first president in July 1969. SASO became the organisational core of the Black Consciousness Movement, which defined "Black" to include Africans, Coloureds, and Indians and argued that oppressed people had to shed internalised inferiority before they could effectively challenge apartheid.

In 1972 Biko helped found the Black People's Convention as a national political umbrella for the movement, and the Black Community Programmes, which ran clinics and self-help projects. That year the University of Natal expelled him for neglecting his studies. In March 1973 the government banned him, confining him to King William's Town and prohibiting him from publishing, speaking publicly, or meeting more than one person at a time. He nonetheless continued to organise, establishing the Zanempilo Community Health Clinic near Ginsberg in 1975.

## Key Achievements

- Founded SASO (1968–69) and served as its first president, reviving Black political organisation during a period of severe repression.
- Articulated Black Consciousness philosophy in a column written under the pseudonym "Frank Talk" for the SASO Newsletter, later collected in *I Write What I Like*.
- Co-founded the Black People's Convention (1972) and Black Community Programmes, linking political ideas to grassroots services.
- Gave testimony in the 1976 SASO/BPC trial that publicly explained Black Consciousness to a national and international audience.
- The movement's ideas shaped the generation of students behind the Soweto uprising of 16 June 1976.

## Controversies & Criticism

The apartheid state characterised Biko as a dangerous agitator, and his death remains the central controversy of his life. Arrested at a roadblock near Grahamstown on 18 August 1977, he was held under the Terrorism Act, interrogated in Room 619 of the Sanlam Building in Port Elizabeth, and suffered brain injuries around 6–7 September. He was transported naked and manacled in the back of a Land Rover roughly 1,100 km to Pretoria, where he died on 12 September. Justice Minister Jimmy Kruger initially suggested Biko had died after a hunger strike. A 1977 inquest found no one criminally responsible. In 1997, five former security policemen applied to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission for amnesty, which was refused in 1999.

Critics of Black Consciousness, including some in the ANC and the South African Communist Party, argued that its focus on race obscured class analysis and that it lacked a concrete strategy for seizing power. Some white liberals regarded Biko's exclusion of whites from Black organisations as a form of separatism, a charge he rejected as a misreading of his aims.

## Legacy & Influence

Biko's funeral on 25 September 1977 drew an estimated 20,000 mourners. Weeks later, on 19 October, the government banned 18 Black Consciousness organisations. Internationally, Donald Woods's book *Biko* (1978) and Richard Attenborough's film *Cry Freedom* (1987) carried his story to a global audience, and Peter Gabriel's song "Biko" (1980) became an anti-apartheid anthem.

The Steve Biko Foundation, established in 1998, promotes his intellectual legacy. His writings continue to influence student movements, including the #RhodesMustFall and #FeesMustFall campaigns of 2015–16, and scholarship on decolonisation and identity.
