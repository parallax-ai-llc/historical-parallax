## Summary

Berry Gordy Jr. (born November 28, 1929) is an American record executive, producer, and songwriter who founded Motown Records in Detroit in 1959. Starting with an $800 family loan, he built the label into the most successful Black-owned business in the United States and one of the most influential record companies of the 20th century.

Motown's roster under Gordy included the Supremes, the Temptations, Marvin Gaye, Stevie Wonder, Smokey Robinson and the Miracles, the Four Tops, and the Jackson 5. Between 1961 and 1971 the company placed 110 singles in the Billboard Top 10, and its "Sound of Young America" carried Black popular music into mainstream white audiences during the civil rights era.

Gordy sold Motown in 1988 and later moved into film and theater. He has been inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame (1988) and the Songwriters Hall of Fame (1990), received the National Medal of Arts (2016), and was a Kennedy Center Honoree (2021).

## Early Life & Background

Gordy was born in Detroit, Michigan, the seventh of eight children of Berry Gordy Sr. and Bertha Fuller Gordy, who had moved north from Georgia in 1922. The family ran small businesses, including a grocery and a plastering firm, and the children were expected to work. Gordy dropped out of Northeastern High School in the eleventh grade to become a professional boxer, fighting as a featherweight before being drafted into the U.S. Army in 1951. He served in the Korean War and earned his high school equivalency before his discharge in 1953.

That year he opened the 3-D Record Mart, a jazz record shop that closed in 1955. He then worked on the assembly line at a Lincoln-Mercury plant, an experience he later cited as the model for Motown's production system.

## Rise to Prominence

Gordy began writing songs in the mid-1950s. With his sister Gwen Gordy and Roquel "Billy" Davis he co-wrote "Reet Petite" (1957) and "Lonely Teardrops" (1958) for Jackie Wilson, the latter reaching number one on the R&B chart. In 1957 he met Smokey Robinson and produced the Miracles' "Got a Job" (1958). Frustrated by small royalty payments, he took Robinson's advice to start his own label.

On January 12, 1959, Gordy founded Tamla Records with a loan from the Gordy family's Ber-Berry Co-op savings fund. Motown Record Corporation was incorporated on April 14, 1960, operating from a house at 2648 West Grand Boulevard that he named "Hitsville U.S.A." Early successes included Barrett Strong's "Money (That's What I Want)" (1959) and the Marvelettes' "Please Mr. Postman," Motown's first pop number one in December 1961.

## Key Achievements

Gordy built Motown as an integrated operation: in-house songwriters such as Holland-Dozier-Holland, the Funk Brothers studio band, a quality-control meeting that voted on every release, and an artist development department that taught etiquette and choreography. The Supremes alone scored twelve number-one pop hits. Gordy also co-wrote hits including "Shop Around," "Do You Love Me," and, as part of The Corporation, the Jackson 5's "I Want You Back" and "ABC."

He moved the company to Los Angeles in 1972 and expanded into film, producing "Lady Sings the Blues" (1972), which received five Academy Award nominations, directing "Mahogany" (1975), and co-producing "The Wiz" (1978). In June 1988 he sold Motown to MCA and Boston Ventures for $61 million, and in 1997 sold half of the Jobete publishing catalog to EMI for $132 million. His memoir "To Be Loved" appeared in 1994, and "Motown: The Musical" opened on Broadway in 2013.

## Controversies & Criticism

Motown's contracts have been widely criticized as restrictive, with artists and musicians receiving low royalties and little ownership. Holland-Dozier-Holland left in 1968 amid lawsuits over compensation. The Funk Brothers went largely uncredited until the 2002 documentary "Standing in the Shadows of Motown." Gordy's decision to remove Florence Ballard from the Supremes in 1967, and his perceived favoritism toward Diana Ross, with whom he had a daughter in 1971, drew lasting criticism. Some artists, including Marvin Gaye, publicly clashed with him over creative control.

## Legacy & Influence

Gordy's assembly-line approach to hitmaking and his insistence on crossover appeal reshaped the music industry and demonstrated the commercial power of Black-owned enterprise. Hitsville U.S.A. became the Motown Museum in 1985. He received a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame in 1996 and announced his retirement at Motown's 60th anniversary celebration in Detroit in September 2019.
