## Summary

Shortly before midnight local time on 31 August 2025, a shallow magnitude 6.0 earthquake struck eastern Afghanistan. It flattened mud-brick villages in Kunar and Nangarhar provinces while people slept [^1][^2]. The Taliban administration reported at least 2,205 deaths and more than 3,600 injuries, which makes it one of the deadliest earthquakes in Afghanistan's modern history [^2]. The disaster arrived as foreign aid was shrinking and as millions of Afghans were returning from Iran and Pakistan. Restrictions on female aid workers became a central concern of the relief effort [^2][^3].

## Background

Eastern Afghanistan lies in a seismically active mountain zone, and the quake was shallow, which intensified shaking at the surface [^1]. People in Kunar typically live in wood and mud-brick houses on steep river valleys, which makes them highly vulnerable to shaking and landslides [^2]. Aid agencies were already stretched before the quake. Afghanistan faced drought and a weak economy, and around 2 million people had returned from neighboring countries, which agencies called "a crisis within a crisis" [^2]. WHO said 80 health facilities had closed because of US aid cuts before the disaster [^3].

## The Event

The quake struck at about 23:47 local time at a depth of roughly 8 km [^1]. Landslides and rockfalls blocked roads, and aid workers had to walk for hours to reach cut-off villages [^2]. An assessment cited by the Associated Press found that about 98 percent of buildings in the hardest-hit areas of Kunar were damaged or destroyed [^2]. A magnitude 5.6 aftershock hit neighboring Nangarhar on 4 September, and dozens of further aftershocks followed [^1][^2]. By 4 September the Taliban's toll had reached 2,205 [^2]. Wikipedia notes that WHO reported a higher figure of nearly 3,000 deaths [^1].

## Response and Aftermath

Taliban authorities, local volunteers, and international agencies evacuated the injured by helicopter and on foot [^1]. The UN released emergency funds and launched an appeal of about $139.6 million [^1]. UNICEF sought $22 million for children and families [^4]. Donors including the European Union, the United Kingdom, China, India, Iran, Pakistan, Qatar, and Russia sent money and supplies [^1]. WHO urged the Taliban to lift restrictions on Afghan women working in aid. Its deputy representative estimated that about 90 percent of medical staff in the affected area were men [^3].

## Positive Perspectives

- Many countries sent relief quickly, including countries that do not formally recognize the Taliban government [^1].
- Local volunteers and rescuers reached remote villages on foot despite landslides [^2].
- UN agencies issued emergency appeals within days, including UNICEF's $22 million request [^4].
- Islamic Relief reported that it had helped more than 28,000 survivors with healthcare, shelter, and livelihoods by the first anniversary [^5].

## Negative Perspectives

- Aid groups were critically underfunded. The Norwegian Refugee Council said it had only $100,000 available for the emergency response [^2].
- WHO said the shortage of female medical staff, caused by Taliban restrictions on women's work and education, kept women from getting care [^3].
- According to Wikipedia, Taliban officials in Nangarhar warned aid groups not to photograph women [^1].
- Earlier US aid cuts had already closed health facilities in the region [^3].
- A UN special rapporteur asked Pakistan to pause deportations after the quake. Pakistan rejected the request [^1].

## Recent News

A year after the quake, Islamic Relief reported that many survivors were still homeless or living in worn-out tents, and that trauma and the loss of livestock were holding back recovery [^5].
