## Summary

The 2025 US federal government shutdown started on 1 October 2025, after Congress failed to pass funding for fiscal year 2026, and ended when President Donald Trump signed a funding bill on 12 November [^1][^2]. At 43 days it was the longest full government shutdown in US history [^1][^2]. The central dispute was over health care. Democrats demanded an extension of enhanced Affordable Care Act (ACA) premium tax credits, while Republicans insisted on passing a funding bill first [^2]. The shutdown disrupted food aid and air travel and left hundreds of thousands of federal workers without pay [^1][^3].

## Background

Government funding under a continuing resolution ran out on 30 September 2025 [^1]. Republicans controlled the House, the Senate, and the White House, but needed Democratic votes to reach the Senate's 60-vote threshold [^1]. Senate Democrats refused to advance a Republican stopgap that did not extend the ACA subsidies due to expire at the end of 2025, and the Senate rejected Republican continuing resolutions 14 times before a deal was reached [^1][^2].

## The Event

About 900,000 federal employees were furloughed and roughly 700,000 others, including air traffic controllers, worked without pay [^1]. The administration laid off about 4,000 federal workers during the shutdown [^1]. The Agriculture Department said it would not issue November benefits under the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). That led to lawsuits, and a federal judge in Rhode Island ordered full November payments. The US Court of Appeals for the First Circuit declined to block that order [^4]. With air traffic controllers working long hours unpaid, the FAA ordered flight capacity cut by 10 percent at 40 major airports starting 7 November, citing safety risks from fatigue [^3]. The Pentagon moved research funds to pay troops and accepted a private donation [^1].

## Response and Aftermath

On 9-10 November, a group of senators negotiated a compromise. Eight members of the Democratic caucus joined Republicans to pass it in the Senate 60-40 [^1]. The House approved it 222-209 on 12 November, and Trump signed it the same day [^2]. The law funded most of the government through 30 January 2026 and gave full-year funding for agriculture and the FDA, veterans affairs and military construction, and the legislative branch. It also reversed shutdown layoffs, guaranteed back pay, and promised Senate Democrats a vote on extending the ACA subsidies [^2]. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said, "This fight is not over" [^2].

## Positive Perspectives

- A bipartisan group of senators broke the deadlock and passed a bill that cleared both chambers [^1][^2].
- The final law guaranteed back pay and reversed layoffs carried out during the shutdown [^2].
- The courts stepped in to keep SNAP food benefits flowing for millions of families [^4].
- The deal funded SNAP for the rest of the fiscal year, which reduces the risk of another lapse in food aid [^1].

## Negative Perspectives

- At 43 days it was the longest shutdown in US history, and federal workers missed multiple paychecks [^2].
- Families lined up at food banks while SNAP benefits were uncertain [^2][^4].
- The FAA's flight cuts stranded travelers and showed how fragile the air traffic control workforce was [^3].
- The deal gave Democrats only a promised Senate vote on the ACA subsidies, not the extension they had demanded, and Jeffries signaled continued conflict [^2].
- Economic data releases were suspended, which left policymakers without key statistics [^1].

## Recent News

The 30 January 2026 deadline brought another lapse. A partial shutdown began on 31 January 2026, and after Congress funded the other agencies, the Department of Homeland Security went unfunded from 13 February 2026 amid a dispute over immigration enforcement [^5].
