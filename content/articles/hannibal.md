## Summary

Hannibal Barca (247–c. 183 BC) was a Carthaginian general who commanded Carthage's forces against Rome in the Second Punic War (218–201 BC). He is best known for marching an army, including war elephants, from Iberia across the Pyrenees and the Alps into northern Italy, where he inflicted a series of crushing defeats on Roman armies over roughly fifteen years of campaigning on Roman soil.

His victories at the Trebia (218 BC), Lake Trasimene (217 BC), and Cannae (216 BC) remain among the most studied battles in military history. Cannae in particular, where his double envelopment destroyed a numerically superior Roman force, is still taught as a model of tactical maneuver.

Despite never being decisively beaten in Italy, Hannibal could not force Rome to surrender. He was recalled to Africa and defeated by Scipio Africanus at Zama in 202 BC. He later served as a Carthaginian statesman and then as an exile advising eastern kings, before taking his own life around 183 BC to avoid capture by Rome.

## Early Life & Background

Hannibal was born in 247 BC in Carthage, in present-day Tunisia, the eldest son of Hamilcar Barca, a leading Carthaginian commander in the First Punic War (264–241 BC). After that war ended in Carthaginian defeat and the loss of Sicily, Hamilcar led an expedition in 237 BC to expand Carthaginian power in the Iberian Peninsula, taking the young Hannibal with him.

Ancient sources, including Polybius and Livy, record the story that Hamilcar made his son swear an oath of eternal enmity toward Rome before departing. Hannibal grew up in military camps in Iberia, receiving both Greek-influenced education and practical training in command. After Hamilcar's death in 228 BC, Hannibal served under his brother-in-law Hasdrubal the Fair, who founded New Carthage (modern Cartagena).

## Rise to Prominence

When Hasdrubal was assassinated in 221 BC, the army proclaimed Hannibal, then about 26, as its commander, a choice the Carthaginian government confirmed. He spent two years consolidating Carthaginian control over Iberian tribes.

In 219 BC he besieged and captured Saguntum, a city allied with Rome, after an eight-month siege. Rome demanded his surrender; Carthage refused, and war was declared in 218 BC. Rather than wait for Roman armies to arrive in Iberia and Africa, Hannibal chose to carry the war into Italy by land. He set out in spring 218 BC with an army that ancient sources put at roughly 90,000 infantry and 12,000 cavalry, though many were left as garrisons or lost en route. He crossed the Rhône, then the Alps in autumn, reaching the Po Valley with perhaps 20,000 infantry, 6,000 cavalry, and a handful of surviving elephants.

## Key Achievements

- **Trebia (December 218 BC):** Lured the consul Sempronius Longus across a freezing river and destroyed a large part of his army with an ambush led by his brother Mago.
- **Lake Trasimene (217 BC):** Trapped the army of the consul Flaminius on a lakeshore road in fog, killing around 15,000 Romans, including Flaminius, in what is often called the largest ambush in ancient history.
- **Cannae (2 August 216 BC):** Facing a Roman army of about 80,000 with roughly 50,000 men, he allowed his center to bend back while his African infantry and cavalry enclosed the Romans on all sides. Roman losses were between 50,000 and 70,000 killed or captured, one of the bloodiest single days of battle ever recorded.
- **Sustained campaign:** Maintained an army in hostile Italian territory from 218 to 203 BC without significant reinforcement from Carthage, winning over Capua, Tarentum, and other Italian cities.
- **Political reform:** As suffete (chief magistrate) of Carthage in 196 BC, he reformed public finances and curbed the power of the oligarchic council, enabling Carthage to pay its war indemnity to Rome without new taxes.

## Controversies & Criticism

Roman writers portrayed Hannibal as treacherous and cruel, a charge modern historians regard as partly propaganda, though his campaigns did involve severe reprisals against communities that defied him. His conduct at Saguntum, where the population was killed or enslaved, was cited by Rome as the war's justification.

Strategically, critics from antiquity onward have questioned why he did not march on Rome immediately after Cannae. Livy records the cavalry commander Maharbal's rebuke that Hannibal knew how to win a victory but not how to use one. Defenders note that he lacked siege equipment and that Rome's Latin allies largely stayed loyal, undermining his plan to break the Roman alliance system.

His political enemies in Carthage later accused him of intriguing with Antiochus III of the Seleucid Empire, forcing him into exile in 195 BC.

## Legacy & Influence

Hannibal's crossing of the Alps and his battlefield tactics have influenced generals from Scipio, who adapted his methods to defeat him, to Napoleon and Alfred von Schlieffen, whose German war plan of 1905 explicitly invoked Cannae. Military academies continue to study his use of terrain, deception, and cavalry.

Rome's eventual victory allowed it to dominate the western Mediterranean, and the fear he inspired persisted in the Roman phrase "Hannibal ad portas" ("Hannibal at the gates"). He remains a symbol of resistance against overwhelming power and a central figure in the history of ancient warfare.
