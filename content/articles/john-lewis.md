## Summary

John Robert Lewis (February 21, 1940 – July 17, 2020) was an American civil rights leader and politician who served in the United States House of Representatives for Georgia's 5th congressional district from 1987 until his death. He was one of the "Big Six" leaders who organized the 1963 March on Washington and, at 23, its youngest speaker.

As chairman of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) from 1963 to 1966, Lewis helped lead the Nashville sit-ins, the Freedom Rides, and the 1965 Selma voting rights marches. On March 7, 1965, known as "Bloody Sunday," Alabama state troopers fractured his skull on the Edmund Pettus Bridge, an event that accelerated passage of the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Over 33 years in Congress, Lewis became known as "the conscience of the Congress." He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2011, and his phrase "good trouble" became shorthand for principled civil disobedience.

## Early Life & Background

Lewis was born near Troy, Alabama, the third of ten children of sharecroppers Eddie and Willie Mae Lewis. As a boy he preached to the family's chickens and was denied a library card because of his race. Inspired by radio broadcasts of Martin Luther King Jr., he wrote to King in 1957 and met him in 1958.

He enrolled at American Baptist Theological Seminary in Nashville in 1957, where he studied nonviolence under James Lawson. He later earned a bachelor's degree in religion and philosophy from Fisk University in 1967.

## Rise to Prominence

In February 1960, Lewis joined the Nashville lunch counter sit-ins and was arrested for the first time. In 1961 he became one of the original 13 Freedom Riders and was beaten in Rock Hill, South Carolina, and in Montgomery, Alabama. He was arrested more than 40 times during the movement.

Elected SNCC chairman in June 1963, he spoke at the March on Washington on August 28, 1963, after other organizers pressed him to soften a draft criticizing the Kennedy administration's civil rights bill. He led the first Selma march in 1965 alongside Hosea Williams.

After leaving SNCC in 1966, he directed the Voter Education Project from 1970 to 1977, helping register nearly four million voters. He served on the Atlanta City Council from 1981 to 1986 before winning his House seat in 1986.

## Key Achievements

- Co-organized the March on Washington and led the Bloody Sunday march that spurred the Voting Rights Act of 1965.
- Served 17 terms in Congress and rose to Senior Chief Deputy Whip in the Democratic caucus.
- Sponsored legislation creating the National Museum of African American History and Culture, which opened in 2016 after he introduced bills for 15 years.
- Received the Presidential Medal of Freedom from Barack Obama on February 15, 2011.
- Co-authored the graphic memoir trilogy *March* (2013–2016); *March: Book Three* won the 2016 National Book Award for Young People's Literature.
- Became the first Black lawmaker to lie in state in the Capitol Rotunda in July 2020.

## Controversies & Criticism

Lewis's 1986 Democratic primary runoff against fellow activist Julian Bond was bitter. Lewis challenged Bond to take a drug test and suggested he was the only candidate never to have used drugs, a tactic critics called divisive. The two remained estranged for years.

In January 2017 he called Donald Trump "not a legitimate president," citing Russian interference, and boycotted the inauguration. Trump responded that Lewis was "all talk" and his district was "in horrible shape," drawing bipartisan rebukes. Lewis had said it was his first missed inauguration, but he had also skipped George W. Bush's in 2001.

Some progressive critics viewed his later career as too close to the Democratic establishment, and he faced questions in 2008 for endorsing Hillary Clinton before switching to Obama.

## Legacy & Influence

Lewis died of pancreatic cancer on July 17, 2020, at age 80, seven months after his diagnosis. A posthumous essay published in *The New York Times* on the day of his funeral urged readers to "redeem the soul of our nation."

Congress named the John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act for him, and campaigns emerged to rename the Edmund Pettus Bridge in his honor. Georgia, his adopted state, elected its first Black senator, Raphael Warnock, six months after his death. His documentary *John Lewis: Good Trouble* (2020) and the *March* books remain widely taught.
