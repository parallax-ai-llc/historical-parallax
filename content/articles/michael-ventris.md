## Summary

Michael George Francis Ventris (12 July 1922 – 6 September 1956) was a British architect and self-taught linguist who deciphered Linear B, the Bronze Age script found on clay tablets at Knossos, Pylos and other Aegean sites. In June 1952 he demonstrated that the script recorded an early form of Greek, pushing the documented history of the Greek language back roughly 500 years before Homer.

Ventris had no formal training in classics or philology. He worked on the problem in his spare time while qualifying and practising as an architect, building on the sign lists of Emmett L. Bennett Jr. and the structural analysis of Alice Kober. His result was confirmed within a year and reshaped the study of Mycenaean civilisation.

He died in a road accident at the age of 34, weeks before his major book with John Chadwick, *Documents in Mycenaean Greek*, was published.

## Early Life & Background

Ventris was born in Wheathampstead, Hertfordshire. His father, Edward Ventris, was a British Army officer who had served in India; his mother, Dorothea Janasz, came from a wealthy Polish family. He spent part of his childhood in Switzerland, where he picked up French, German and Swiss German, and he learned Polish from his mother.

He attended Stowe School from 1935. In 1936, aged 14, he visited an exhibition at Burlington House marking the 50th anniversary of the British School at Athens, where Sir Arthur Evans spoke about the undeciphered Minoan scripts. The encounter fixed his interest for the rest of his life. In 1940, at 18, he published "Introducing the Minoan Language" in the *American Journal of Archaeology*, arguing (wrongly, as he later admitted) that Linear B was related to Etruscan.

He trained at the Architectural Association School in London, served as a navigator in RAF Bomber Command from 1942, and qualified as an architect in 1948. He married Lois Knox-Niven in 1942; they had two children.

## Rise to Prominence

From 1950 Ventris pursued Linear B systematically. That year he circulated a questionnaire to leading scholars and compiled their replies as the "Mid-Century Report". Between January 1951 and June 1952 he distributed 20 "Work Notes" to about two dozen specialists, recording his evolving analysis.

His method combined Kober's discovery that the language was inflected with a grid that assigned each sign a consonant and vowel value. Place names such as Knossos and Amnisos, read from the grid, forced him to test Greek, which he had long resisted. On 1 July 1952 he announced on the BBC Third Programme that Linear B was Greek.

The Cambridge philologist John Chadwick heard the broadcast and offered his help. Their joint paper, "Evidence for Greek Dialect in the Mycenaean Archives", appeared in the *Journal of Hellenic Studies* in 1953.

## Key Achievements

- **Decipherment of Linear B (1952):** established that Mycenaean administrators wrote Greek in the 15th to 13th centuries BC.
- **Independent confirmation (1953):** Carl Blegen read a newly excavated Pylos tablet, listing tripods and vessels, using Ventris's values; the words matched the pictograms.
- ***Documents in Mycenaean Greek* (1956):** a 452-page edition of 300 tablets with Chadwick, published by Cambridge University Press.
- **Honours:** appointed OBE in 1955 and awarded an honorary doctorate by Uppsala University.

## Controversies & Criticism

A minority of scholars rejected the decipherment. A. J. Beattie of Edinburgh argued in 1956 that the results were circular and that Ventris had fitted Greek to the grid after the fact. The Pylos tripod tablet, excavated after the grid was fixed, answered that objection for most specialists, though some dissent persisted into the 1960s.

Later writers, notably Margalit Fox in *The Riddle of the Labyrinth* (2013), argued that Alice Kober's foundational work received too little public credit, even though Ventris himself acknowledged it. Ventris's rushed and unpolished architectural career has also been noted; he left few completed buildings.

## Legacy & Influence

The decipherment created the field of Mycenaean studies and clarified the relationship between Minoan Crete and mainland Greece. Ventris's grid method remains a model for attacking unknown scripts. His house in Hampstead, which he designed, carries a blue plaque, and the Institute of Classical Studies awards the annual Michael Ventris Award for Mycenaean studies and architecture. Andrew Robinson's 2002 biography, *The Man Who Deciphered Linear B*, remains the standard account.
