## Summary

Ella Josephine Baker (December 13, 1903 – December 13, 1986) was an American civil rights and human rights activist whose organizing career spanned more than five decades. She worked behind the scenes with the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP), the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), and the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), and she mentored a generation of activists including Diane Nash, Bob Moses, Julian Bond, and Stokely Carmichael.

Baker is best known for her philosophy of participatory democracy and group-centered leadership, summarized in her maxim that "strong people don't need strong leaders." She rejected the charismatic, minister-led model that dominated the movement in the 1950s and instead pressed for grassroots organizing that developed ordinary people's capacity to lead themselves. That approach shaped SNCC, the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, and later community-organizing traditions.

## Early Life & Background

Baker was born in Norfolk, Virginia, and moved with her family in 1910 to Littleton, North Carolina, where her mother had grown up. Her grandmother, who had been enslaved, told her stories of resistance, including being whipped for refusing to marry a man chosen by the slaveholder. These accounts, along with her mother's activity in Baptist missionary circles, shaped Baker's early sense of justice.

She attended Shaw University in Raleigh, North Carolina, where she challenged school policies she considered unfair, and graduated as class valedictorian in 1927. She then moved to New York City, arriving just before the Great Depression. In Harlem she wrote for the *West Indian News* and the *Negro National News*, and in 1930 she joined the Young Negroes' Cooperative League, founded by George Schuyler, becoming its national director in 1931. She also worked for the Works Progress Administration's Workers' Education Project, teaching consumer education and labor history.

## Rise to Prominence

In 1940 Baker joined the NAACP as a field secretary, traveling extensively through the South to recruit members and build local branches. She was promoted to national director of branches in 1943, making her the organization's highest-ranking woman. Frustrated by the NAACP's top-down bureaucracy, she resigned from the national staff in 1946, though she later served as president of the New York City branch from 1952 to 1953, the first woman to hold that post.

In 1956, with Bayard Rustin and Stanley Levison, Baker co-founded In Friendship to raise money for Southern activists facing economic reprisals after the Montgomery bus boycott. In January 1958 she moved to Atlanta to organize the SCLC's Crusade for Citizenship voter registration campaign and served as the organization's first, though officially "acting," executive director until 1960.

## Key Achievements

Baker's most consequential act came in April 1960. After the Greensboro sit-ins spread across the South, she used $800 from the SCLC to convene roughly 200 student activists at Shaw University over Easter weekend. She urged them to remain independent of established organizations rather than become a youth wing of the SCLC. The result was the founding of SNCC, which became the movement's most militant and democratic wing.

Baker left the SCLC in August 1960 and worked for the Southern Conference Educational Fund from 1962 to 1967. In 1964 she helped organize the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP) and delivered the keynote address at its state convention in Jackson before the party challenged the all-white Mississippi delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City. In the 1970s she supported the Puerto Rican independence movement, the Free Angela Davis campaign, and anti-apartheid efforts.

## Controversies & Criticism

Baker's tenure at the SCLC was marked by conflict. She openly criticized the organization's dependence on Martin Luther King Jr.'s celebrity and clashed with ministers who, in her account, were unaccustomed to taking direction from a woman. King and his allies, in turn, regarded her as difficult and never gave her the permanent executive director title.

Some historians and contemporaries have questioned whether her strict anti-hierarchical model was workable at scale. SNCC's consensus-driven structure produced long, exhausting meetings and, by the late 1960s, internal fractures over Black Power and the role of white members that the organization did not survive. Her relatively low public profile during her lifetime has also been attributed partly to the sexism of the movement's leadership and partly to her own choice to avoid the spotlight.

## Legacy & Influence

Baker died in New York City on her 83rd birthday in 1986. Her ideas outlived the organizations she built. The Ella Baker Center for Human Rights, founded in Oakland in 1996, and the Ella Baker School in New York carry her name. Barbara Ransby's 2003 biography, *Ella Baker and the Black Freedom Movement*, and Joanne Grant's 1981 documentary *Fundi: The Story of Ella Baker* helped restore her place in civil rights history. Her 1964 speech inspired the song "Ella's Song" by Sweet Honey in the Rock, whose refrain, "We who believe in freedom cannot rest until it comes," remains an anthem of grassroots organizing. In 2009 she was honored with a United States postage stamp.
