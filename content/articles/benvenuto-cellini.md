## Summary

Benvenuto Cellini (3 November 1500 – 13 February 1571) was a Florentine goldsmith, sculptor, and writer of the Italian Mannerist period. He worked for Pope Clement VII, King Francis I of France, and Duke Cosimo I de' Medici, and produced two of the most celebrated objects of the sixteenth century: the gold Saltcellar made for Francis I and the bronze *Perseus with the Head of Medusa* in Florence's Loggia dei Lanzi.

Cellini is equally remembered for his autobiography, the *Vita*, dictated between 1558 and 1563. Boastful, violent, and vivid, it is one of the most widely read self-portraits of the Renaissance and shaped how later generations imagined the artist as a temperamental individual. His technical treatises on goldsmithing and sculpture, published in 1568, remain primary sources for Renaissance workshop practice.

## Early Life & Background

Cellini was born in Florence, the son of Giovanni Cellini, a musician and instrument maker who wanted his son to become a flautist. Benvenuto resisted and at age 15 was apprenticed to the goldsmith Antonio di Sandro. In 1516 he was banished from Florence for six months after a brawl and worked in Siena, Bologna, and Pisa before returning. In 1519 he moved to Rome, where he made vases, medals, and jewelry and gained the patronage of Pope Clement VII.

## Rise to Prominence

During the Sack of Rome in 1527, Cellini served as a gunner in the Castel Sant'Angelo and later claimed to have shot the Constable of Bourbon and wounded the Prince of Orange. He became a master of the papal mint and designed coins and medals for Clement VII and, later, Paul III. In 1538 he was imprisoned in Castel Sant'Angelo on charges of stealing papal jewels during the sack; he escaped, was recaptured, and was released in 1539 through the intervention of Cardinal Ippolito d'Este.

In 1540 he entered the service of Francis I of France at Fontainebleau. He returned to Florence in 1545 and spent the rest of his career under Cosimo I de' Medici.

## Key Achievements

- **Saltcellar (Saliera)**, 1540–1543: a gold and enamel table piece for Francis I depicting Neptune and Earth, now in the Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna. It was stolen in 2003 and recovered in 2006.
- **Nymph of Fontainebleau**, 1542–1543: a large bronze relief made for the palace gateway, now in the Louvre.
- **Perseus with the Head of Medusa**, 1545–1554: a bronze over three meters tall commissioned by Cosimo I. Cellini's account of the casting, when he threw his pewter dishes into the furnace to save the pour, is a famous passage of the *Vita*.
- **Bust of Cosimo I**, 1545–1548, and the marble **Crucifix**, 1556–1562, now at the Escorial in Spain.
- **Writings**: the *Vita*, first printed in 1728, and the *Treatises on Goldsmithing and Sculpture*, published in 1568.

## Controversies & Criticism

Cellini killed at least two men, including the goldsmith Pompeo de Capitaneis in Rome in 1534, and received a papal pardon. He was charged with sodomy in 1523 and again in 1557, when a Florentine court sentenced him to four years in prison, commuted to house arrest. Historians treat the *Vita* with caution because Cellini exaggerates his exploits and settles scores with rivals such as Baccio Bandinelli. His relationship with Cosimo I soured over payment disputes for the *Perseus*.

## Legacy & Influence

Cellini died in Florence on 13 February 1571 and was buried in the church of Santissima Annunziata. Goethe translated the *Vita* into German in 1796, and Hector Berlioz based his 1838 opera *Benvenuto Cellini* on it. The *Perseus* remains one of the defining works of Florentine Mannerism, and the Saltcellar is often described as the most important surviving piece of Renaissance goldsmithing.
