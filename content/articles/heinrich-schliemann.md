## Summary

Heinrich Schliemann (1822–1890) was a German businessman and self-taught archaeologist who became famous for excavating Hisarlik in northwestern Anatolia, the site now widely accepted as ancient Troy, and for his discoveries at Mycenae in Greece. He built a large fortune in trade before turning to archaeology in his late forties, driven by a lifelong conviction that Homer's epics described real places and events.

His excavations between 1870 and 1890 uncovered multiple superimposed settlement layers at Hisarlik, the treasure he named "Priam's Treasure" in 1873, and the shaft graves and gold funerary masks at Mycenae in 1876. These finds pushed the known history of the Aegean back into the Bronze Age and helped establish Mycenaean civilization as a field of study.

Schliemann remains a divisive figure. He is credited with bringing Bronze Age Greece and Anatolia into modern scholarship, yet his methods destroyed important evidence, and his own accounts of his life and finds contain documented fabrications.

## Early Life & Background

Schliemann was born on January 6, 1822, in Neubukow, in the Grand Duchy of Mecklenburg-Schwerin, the son of a Protestant pastor. His mother died in 1831, and financial scandal surrounding his father cut short his formal education. At 14 he was apprenticed to a grocer in Fürstenberg, where he worked for about five years.

In 1841 he sailed for Venezuela as a cabin boy, but the ship wrecked off the Dutch coast. He found work in Amsterdam, first as an office messenger and then as a bookkeeper. Schliemann taught himself languages with unusual speed, eventually claiming fluency in more than a dozen, including Russian, Greek, and Arabic. In 1846 his employer sent him to Saint Petersburg as an agent, where he began trading on his own account, initially in indigo and later in commodities.

## Rise to Prominence

Schliemann's wealth came from several ventures. During the California Gold Rush he ran a bank in Sacramento in 1851–1852, buying gold dust from miners. He became a United States citizen, though the date and circumstances he gave were later disputed. Back in Russia he profited heavily as a military contractor during the Crimean War (1853–1856), supplying saltpetre, sulphur, and lead. By 1858 he could retire from business.

He studied briefly at the Sorbonne in 1866 and traveled widely. In 1868 he toured Greece and the Troad, where the British expatriate Frank Calvert, who owned part of the Hisarlik mound, persuaded him that it was the site of Troy. Schliemann divorced his Russian wife in 1869 and married Sophia Engastromenos, a 17-year-old Greek woman, that same year.

## Key Achievements

- **Troy (1870–1890):** Schliemann excavated Hisarlik in campaigns from 1871 to 1873, 1878 to 1879, 1882, and 1889 to 1890. He identified nine major settlement layers. In May 1873 he uncovered a cache of gold and silver objects he called "Priam's Treasure" and assigned to Homer's Troy, though it belonged to Troy II, roughly a thousand years too early.
- **Mycenae (1876):** Digging inside the Lion Gate, he found Grave Circle A with five shaft graves containing gold death masks, weapons, and jewelry. He telegraphed that he had "gazed upon the face of Agamemnon"; the graves date to about 1600–1500 BC, centuries before any Trojan War.
- **Other sites:** He excavated Orchomenos in 1880 and Tiryns in 1884–1885, where he worked with the architect Wilhelm Dörpfeld, whose stratigraphic methods improved later seasons at Troy.
- **Publications:** *Troy and Its Remains* (1875), *Mycenae* (1878), and *Ilios* (1880) reached wide audiences and attracted scholarly attention to the Aegean Bronze Age.

## Controversies & Criticism

Schliemann's methods were destructive even by the standards of his time. His deep trench through Hisarlik cut away much of the layer (Troy VI–VIIa) that later archaeologists associated with Homeric Troy. He smuggled Priam's Treasure out of the Ottoman Empire in violation of his excavation permit, which led to a lawsuit and a fine of 10,000 gold francs, of which he paid 50,000 to retain the objects.

Scholars including David Traill and William Calder III have shown that his autobiographical writings contain invented episodes, such as a claimed meeting with President Millard Fillmore and an inflated account of a San Francisco fire. His excavation diaries contradict his published claim that Sophia was present when the treasure was found. Some researchers have questioned whether Priam's Treasure was a single deposit or assembled from several finds. He also gave Frank Calvert little credit for identifying Hisarlik.

## Legacy & Influence

Schliemann's finds established that a literate, wealthy Bronze Age civilization preceded classical Greece, laying groundwork for the later work of Arthur Evans at Knossos and Carl Blegen at Troy. Dörpfeld, his assistant, became a leading figure in stratigraphic excavation. Priam's Treasure, taken from Berlin by Soviet forces in 1945, has been held at the Pushkin Museum in Moscow since its public reappearance in 1993 and remains the subject of a German–Russian restitution dispute.

He died in Naples on December 26, 1890, after an ear operation, and was buried in Athens in a mausoleum modeled on a Greek temple. His Athens residence, the Iliou Melathron, now houses the Numismatic Museum of Greece.
