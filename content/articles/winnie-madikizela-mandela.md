## Summary

Winnie Madikizela-Mandela (1936–2018) was a South African anti-apartheid activist and politician who became one of the most visible figures of the liberation struggle during the 27 years her husband, Nelson Mandela, spent in prison. Banned, detained, held in solitary confinement and banished to a rural town, she refused to withdraw from politics and became known to supporters as the "Mother of the Nation."

Her legacy is contested. She was convicted in 1991 of kidnapping in connection with the killing of a 14-year-old activist, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission later held her accountable for abuses committed by her bodyguards. She nonetheless retained a mass following, served in Parliament for most of the post-1994 era, and remains a symbol of both the defiance and the moral cost of the struggle against apartheid.

## Early Life & Background

Nomzamo Winifred Zanyiwe Madikizela was born on 26 September 1936 in Mbongweni village near Bizana, in the Pondoland region of what is now the Eastern Cape. Both her parents, Columbus and Gertrude Madikizela, were teachers. Her mother died when she was about nine years old.

She completed secondary school at Shawbury and moved to Johannesburg to study at the Jan Hofmeyr School of Social Work, graduating in 1955. She then became the first Black medical social worker at Baragwanath Hospital in Soweto, where her casework exposed her to the poverty produced by apartheid policy.

## Rise to Prominence

She met Nelson Mandela, then a lawyer and ANC leader on trial for treason, in 1957. They married on 14 June 1958 and had two daughters, Zenani (born 1959) and Zindziswa (born 1960). After Nelson Mandela was sentenced to life imprisonment in June 1964, she became his public voice and a target of the state.

The government banned her in 1962. In May 1969 she was detained under the Terrorism Act and held for 491 days, most of it in solitary confinement, before being released without conviction in September 1970. Following the 1976 Soweto uprising she was banished in May 1977 to Brandfort, a small town in the Orange Free State, where she lived under restriction until 1985. Her defiance during these years, documented in the 1984 memoir *Part of My Soul Went with Him*, made her an international figure.

## Key Achievements

- Co-founded the Federation of South African Women's activism in the 1950s and helped lead the 1958 anti-pass campaign in Johannesburg.
- Received the Robert F. Kennedy Human Rights Award in 1985 alongside Allan Boesak and Beyers Naudé.
- Elected president of the ANC Women's League in 1993 and re-elected in 1997, serving until 2003.
- Appointed Deputy Minister of Arts, Culture, Science and Technology in 1994 in South Africa's first democratic government.
- Elected to the ANC National Executive Committee in 2007 with the fifth-highest vote and served as a member of Parliament until her death.
- Awarded the Order of Luthuli in Silver in 2016 for her contribution to the liberation struggle.

## Controversies & Criticism

In April 1986 she declared that "with our boxes of matches and our necklaces we shall liberate this country," widely read as an endorsement of necklacing, the burning of suspected collaborators. Her bodyguards, the Mandela United Football Club, were implicated in assaults and killings in Soweto. In January 1989 the 14-year-old activist Stompie Seipei was abducted from a church shelter and murdered. In 1991 she was convicted of kidnapping and being an accessory to assault and sentenced to six years; on appeal in 1993 the accessory conviction was overturned and the sentence reduced to a fine and suspended term.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission concluded in 1998 that she was "politically and morally accountable" for gross human rights violations committed by the club. Nelson Mandela dismissed her from his cabinet in 1995 amid allegations of insubordination and corruption; they separated in 1992 and divorced in March 1996. In 2003 she was convicted on 43 counts of fraud related to a funeral-fund scheme, though the sentence was suspended on appeal in 2004.

## Legacy & Influence

Madikizela-Mandela died in Johannesburg on 2 April 2018, aged 81, and received a state funeral at Orlando Stadium in Soweto on 14 April. Mbizana Local Municipality was renamed in her honour in 2019. Her life continues to divide opinion: to critics she embodied the brutality that the struggle bred within its own ranks, while to many South Africans, particularly younger activists, she represents an uncompromising resistance that the negotiated settlement of 1994 never fully honoured.
